        INTRODUCTION TO WAVES
A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one point to another without the net transfer of matter. Waves may travel through a medium or through a vacuum, depending on their nature.
  Important wave properties:
- Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement from equilibrium.
- Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive points in the same phase.
- Frequency (f): Number of complete oscillations per second (Hz).
- Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (s).
- Wave speed (v): Rate at which the disturbance propagates (m/s).
- Phase: Describes the position of a point in its oscillation cycle.
- Angular frequency (ω): Rate of change of phase (rad/s).
- Wave number (k): Spatial frequency of a wave (rad/m).
  Fundamental equations:
f = 1/T
T = 1/f
v = fλ
ω = 2πf = 2π/T
k = 2π/λ

    CLASSIFICATION OF WAVES
  Mechanical waves
Require a material medium for propagation.
Examples: Sound waves, water waves and waves on strings.
  Electromagnetic waves
Do not require a material medium and can travel through a vacuum.
Examples: Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
  Transverse waves
Particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.
Examples: Waves on a stretched string and electromagnetic waves.
  Longitudinal waves
Particles oscillate parallel to the direction of propagation.
They consist of compressions and rarefactions.
Example: Sound waves in air.
  Progressive waves
Transfer energy continuously through a medium or space.
  Stationary (standing) waves
Form when two identical waves travel in opposite directions and interfere.
They contain:
- Nodes: Points of zero displacement.
- Antinodes: Points of maximum displacement.
There is no net energy transfer along an ideal stationary wave.

    MATHEMATICAL REPRESENTATION OF WAVES
A progressive sinusoidal wave travelling in the positive x-direction is:
    y(x,t) = A sin(kx - ωt + φ)
For a wave travelling in the negative x-direction:
    y(x,t) = A sin(kx + ωt + φ)
Where:
A = Amplitude
k = Wave number
ω = Angular frequency
φ = Initial phase
The phase difference between two points separated by distance Δx:
    Δφ = 2πΔx/λ
Phase difference due to time interval Δt:
    Δφ = 2πΔt/T = ωΔt

    WAVE SPEED AND PROPAGATION
Wave speed depends on the properties of the medium.
For a stretched string:
    v = √(F/μ)
Where:
F = Tension in the string (N)
μ = Linear mass density (kg/m)
    μ = m/L
For sound waves in a fluid:
    v = √(B/ρ)
Where:
B = Bulk modulus (Pa)
ρ = Density of the medium (kg/m³)
For sound in an ideal gas:
    v = √(γRT/M)
Where:
γ = Ratio of specific heat capacities
R = Universal gas constant
T = Absolute temperature (K)
M = Molar mass (kg/mol)
For sound in air, speed generally increases with temperature.

    PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION
When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of their individual displacements.
    y_resultant = y₁ + y₂ + ... + yₙ
This principle explains interference, beats and standing waves.

    INTERFERENCE OF WAVES
Interference occurs when two or more coherent waves overlap and combine.
Coherent sources have:
- The same frequency.
- A constant phase difference.
  Constructive interference
Occurs when waves meet in phase, producing maximum resultant amplitude.
Path difference:
    Δx = nλ
Phase difference:
    Δφ = 2nπ
  Destructive interference
Occurs when waves meet out of phase, producing minimum resultant amplitude.
Path difference:
    Δx = (n + 1/2)λ
Phase difference:
    Δφ = (2n + 1)π
Where n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
For two waves of equal amplitude A:
- Constructive resultant amplitude = 2A.
- Destructive resultant amplitude = 0.
For unequal amplitudes, complete cancellation does not occur.

    BEATS
Beats occur when two waves of slightly different frequencies interfere, producing periodic variations in loudness or amplitude.
Beat frequency:
    fᵦ = |f₁ - f₂|
Beat period:
    Tᵦ = 1/fᵦ
Applications:
- Tuning musical instruments.
- Comparing frequencies.
- Detecting small frequency differences.

    STANDING WAVES AND RESONANCE
Standing waves form due to interference between two waves of equal frequency, amplitude and speed travelling in opposite directions.
For a string fixed at both ends:
    L = nλ/2
    λₙ = 2L/n
    fₙ = nv/2L
Where n = 1, 2, 3, ...
Fundamental frequency:
    f₁ = v/2L
Harmonics:
- First harmonic: Fundamental frequency.
- Second harmonic: 2f₁.
- Third harmonic: 3f₁.
For an open pipe (both ends open):
    fₙ = nv/2L
For a pipe closed at one end:
    fₙ = (2n - 1)v/4L
Only odd harmonics occur in an ideal closed pipe.
 Resonance:
Occurs when a system is driven at or near its natural frequency, resulting in a large-amplitude oscillation.
 Applications:
- Musical instruments.
- Radio tuning.
- Mechanical vibration analysis.
- Structural engineering.

    SOUND WAVES
Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave produced by vibrating objects.
 Important properties:
- Pitch depends mainly on frequency.
- Loudness is related to sound intensity and perception.
- Quality (timbre) depends on waveform and harmonic content.
Sound intensity:
    I = P/A
For a point source radiating uniformly:
    I = P/4πr²
Intensity level:
    β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀)
Where:
    I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² (reference intensity in air).
 Doppler effect:
The apparent change in frequency caused by relative motion between a source and an observer.
For sound:
    f' = f(v ± vₒ)/(v ∓ vₛ)
Where:
f' = Observed frequency
f = Source frequency
v = Speed of sound
vₒ = Observer speed
vₛ = Source speed
Use signs according to whether the source and observer move towards or away from each other. Approaching increases observed frequency; separating decreases it.
 Applications:
- Radar and speed measurement.
- Medical ultrasound.
- Acoustic measurement.
- Astronomy.

INTRODUCTION TO OPTICS
Optics is the branch of physics concerned with the behaviour, propagation and interaction of light with matter.
 Main branches:
-Geometrical (ray) optics:
Treats light as rays travelling in straight lines in a uniform medium. Explains reflection, refraction, mirrors and lenses.
-Physical (wave) optics:
Treats light as a wave. Explains interference, diffraction and polarization.
-Quantum optics:
Explains light in terms of photons and their interactions with matter.

    NATURE AND PROPERTIES OF LIGHT
Light is an electromagnetic transverse wave that can travel through a vacuum.
Speed of light in vacuum:
    c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s
Wave equation:
    c = fλ
For light in a medium:
    v = c/n
Where n = Refractive index.
 Refractive index:
    n = c/v
Frequency remains constant when light passes from one medium to another, but its speed and wavelength change.
 Electromagnetic spectrum (increasing frequency):
Radio waves → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays.
Visible light approximately ranges from 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red).

    REFLECTION OF LIGHT
Reflection occurs when light strikes a surface and returns into the original medium.
 Laws of reflection:
1. Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.
2. Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
    θᵢ = θᵣ
Types:
- Regular reflection: Occurs on smooth surfaces and produces clear images.
- Diffuse reflection: Occurs on rough surfaces and scatters light in different directions.
 PLANE MIRRORS:
- Produce virtual, upright images.
- Image size equals object size.
- Image distance equals object distance.
- Produce lateral inversion.
 SPHERICAL MIRRORS:
-Concave mirror:
Converges parallel incident rays towards a focal point.
-Convex mirror:
Diverges reflected rays and has a virtual focal point.
Mirror equation:
    1/f = 1/u + 1/v
Magnification:
    m = hᵢ/hₒ = -v/u
Radius of curvature:
    f = R/2
Where:
f = Focal length
u = Object distance
v = Image distance
hᵢ = Image height
hₒ = Object height
Use a consistent Cartesian sign convention when applying mirror equations.

    REFRACTION OF LIGHT
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium into another due to a change in speed.
 Snell's law:
    n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
Where:
n₁, n₂ = Refractive indices
θ₁, θ₂ = Angles measured from the normal.
 Relative refractive index:
    n₂₁ = n₂/n₁ = v₁/v₂
When light enters a denser optical medium:
- Its speed decreases.
- Its wavelength decreases.
- It bends towards the normal.
When light enters a less optically dense medium:
- Its speed increases.
- Its wavelength increases.
- It bends away from the normal.

    TOTAL INTERNAL REFLECTION (TIR)
Occurs when light travels from an optically denser medium to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.
 Conditions:
1. Light must travel from higher refractive index to lower refractive index.
2. Angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.
 Critical angle:
    sin C = n₂/n₁
For a medium-to-air boundary:
    sin C = 1/n
Applications:
- Optical fibres.
- Medical endoscopes.
- Prisms in optical instruments.
- Telecommunications.

    DISPERSION OF LIGHT
Dispersion is the separation of white light into its constituent colours due to the dependence of refractive index on wavelength.
In a glass prism:
- Violet light generally deviates more.
- Red light generally deviates less.
The spectrum consists of:
Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange and Red (VIBGYOR).
 Applications:
- Spectroscopy.
- Optical instruments.
- Analysis of light sources.
- Astronomy.

    LENSES
A lens is a transparent optical device that refracts light to form images.
Types:
 Convex (converging) lens:
Thicker at the centre than at the edges.
Converges parallel rays towards a real focal point.
 Concave (diverging) lens:
Thinner at the centre than at the edges.
Diverges parallel rays as though they originated from a virtual focal point.
Thin lens equation:
    1/f = 1/u + 1/v
Magnification:
    m = hᵢ/hₒ = -v/u
Power of a lens:
    P = 1/f
Where f is in metres and P is in dioptres (D).
Lens maker's formula:
    1/f = (n - 1)(1/R₁ - 1/R₂)
For a thin lens in air, using the standard Cartesian radius sign convention.
Combination of thin lenses in contact:
    1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂
    P = P₁ + P₂

    IMAGE FORMATION BY LENSES
CONVEX LENS:
- Object beyond 2F: Real, inverted, diminished image between F and 2F.
- Object at 2F: Real, inverted, same-size image at 2F.
- Object between F and 2F: Real, inverted, magnified image beyond 2F.
- Object at F: Image at infinity.
- Object between F and the lens: Virtual, upright, magnified image.
CONCAVE LENS:
For a real object, it always produces a virtual, upright and diminished image between the lens and its focal point.

OPTICAL INSTRUMENTS
 Simple microscope:
Uses a convex lens to produce a magnified virtual image.
 Compound microscope:
Uses two converging lenses:
- Objective lens forms an enlarged real intermediate image.
- Eyepiece magnifies the intermediate image.
 Astronomical telescope:
Uses an objective lens of long focal length and an eyepiece of short focal length.
For normal adjustment:
    M = -fₒ/fₑ
Where:
fₒ = Objective focal length
fₑ = Eyepiece focal length
The negative sign indicates image inversion.
 Human eye:
The eye focuses light onto the retina using its optical system.
Common defects:
- Myopia (short-sightedness): Corrected using a concave lens.
- Hypermetropia (long-sightedness): Corrected using a convex lens.
- Presbyopia: Age-related reduction in accommodation; commonly corrected using suitable converging or multifocal lenses.
- Astigmatism: Corrected using cylindrical or toric lenses.

    INTERFERENCE OF LIGHT
Light interference occurs when coherent light waves overlap and produce regions of maximum and minimum intensity.
Young's double-slit experiment demonstrates the wave nature of light.
Bright fringe condition:
    d sin θ = mλ
Dark fringe condition:
    d sin θ = (m + 1/2)λ
For small angles:
Fringe width:
    β = λD/d
Where:
β = Distance between consecutive bright or dark fringes
λ = Wavelength
D = Distance from slits to screen
d = Separation between slits
The fringe width increases with wavelength and screen distance, and decreases with slit separation.
 Applications:
- Measuring wavelength.
- Precision optical measurements.
- Interferometers.
- Thin-film analysis.

    DIFFRACTION OF LIGHT
Diffraction is the bending and spreading of waves when they pass through an aperture or around an obstacle.
It is significant when the aperture or obstacle dimension is comparable to the wavelength.
Single-slit diffraction minima:
    a sin θ = mλ
Where:
a = Slit width
m = 1, 2, 3, ...
For small angles, the width of the central maximum on a distant screen is approximately:
    W = 2λD/a
Diffraction is more pronounced for longer wavelengths and narrower apertures.
 Applications:
- Diffraction gratings.
- Spectroscopy.
- Optical resolution.
- Analysis of crystal structures using X-rays.

    DIFFRACTION GRATINGS
A diffraction grating consists of many equally spaced slits that produce sharp interference maxima.
Grating equation:
    d sin θ = mλ
Where:
d = Grating spacing
θ = Diffraction angle
m = Order of diffraction
λ = Wavelength
If there are N lines per unit length:
    d = 1/N
Resolving power:
    R = λ/Δλ = mN
Where N is the total number of illuminated grating lines.
Applications:
- Spectrometers.
- Wavelength measurement.
- Identification of spectral lines.

    POLARIZATION OF LIGHT
Polarization is the restriction of the oscillations of a transverse wave to a particular direction or plane.
Ordinary unpolarized light contains electric-field oscillations in many transverse directions.
Types:
- Linear polarization.
- Circular polarization.
- Elliptical polarization.
Malus's law:
    I = I₀ cos² θ
Where:
I₀ = Intensity of polarized light incident on the analyser.
θ = Angle between transmission axes.
For unpolarized light passing through an ideal polarizer:
    I = I₀/2
Brewster's law:
    tan θᵦ = n₂/n₁
At Brewster's angle, reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular for non-absorbing media. 
 Applications:
- LCD screens.
- Glare reduction.
- Stress analysis.
- Optical communication.
- Polarizing filters.

ENERGY AND MOMENTUM OF LIGHT
Photon energy:
    E = hf = hc/λ
Photon momentum:
    p = h/λ
Where:
    h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
Light carries both energy and momentum and can exert radiation pressure.
This is evidence of the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.

    OPTICAL FIBRES
Optical fibres transmit light through a core surrounded by a lower-refractive-index cladding.
Working principle:
Total internal reflection.
Numerical aperture (NA):
    NA = √(n₁² - n₂²)
For a fibre in air, assuming the usual step-index geometry.
Where:
n₁ = Core refractive index
n₂ = Cladding refractive index
Acceptance angle:
    NA = sin θₐ
For a fibre in air.
Advantages:
- High bandwidth.
- Low transmission losses.
- Immunity to electromagnetic interference.
- Small size and weight.
- Electrical isolation.
 Applications:
- Telecommunications.
- Internet data transmission.
- Medical endoscopy.
- Industrial sensing.

IMPORTANT OPTICAL AND WAVE APPLICATIONS IN ENGINEERING
- Acoustics: Noise control, sound insulation and architectural design.
- Ultrasonics: Medical imaging, flaw detection and distance measurement.
- Optical communication: High-speed data transmission through fibre optics.
- Lasers: Precision measurement, cutting, welding and alignment.
- Spectroscopy: Material identification and chemical analysis.
- Interferometry: Precision displacement and surface measurements.
- Imaging systems: Cameras, microscopes, telescopes and sensors.
- Radar and Doppler systems: Motion and velocity measurement.
- Holography: Three-dimensional imaging and optical data storage.

EXAMINATION TIPS AND IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS
1. Always distinguish frequency, wavelength, period and wave speed.
2. Remember that frequency remains constant during refraction, while speed and wavelength change.
3. Measure angles of incidence and refraction from the normal, not the surface.
4. Total internal reflection only occurs when light travels from a higher to a lower refractive index.
5. Distinguish interference (superposition of coherent waves) from diffraction (wave spreading at apertures or obstacles).
6. A real image can be projected onto a screen; a virtual image cannot.
7. Use a consistent sign convention for mirrors and lenses.
8. Convert nanometres to metres before substituting into SI equations:
   1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.
9. Distinguish between wave intensity and amplitude:
   For a sinusoidal wave in the same medium, intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.
10. For standing waves, identify nodes, antinodes and boundary conditions before applying harmonic equations.
11. In Young's double-slit experiment, fringe width is β = λD/d.
12. In diffraction, narrower slits produce greater spreading.
13. In polarization, Malus's law applies to already polarized incident light.
14. Always include units in numerical answers and check dimensional consistency.
15. Draw labelled ray diagrams for mirror, lens and optical instrument problems.
