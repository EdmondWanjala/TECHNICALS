                THERMODYNAMICS
Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies heat, temperature, work, energy and their transformations in physical systems.
It explains how energy is transferred and converted, particularly in thermal, mechanical and engineering systems.
 Important applications:
- Heat engines and power plants.
- Refrigerators and air conditioning.
- Internal combustion engines.
- Boilers and turbines.
- Heat exchangers.
- Thermal management of electronic systems.
- Renewable energy systems.

BASIC THERMODYNAMIC CONCEPTS
 System:
A specified quantity of matter or region in space selected for study.
 Surroundings:
Everything outside the thermodynamic system.
 Boundary:
The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings.
 Universe:
The system and its surroundings.
 Types of thermodynamic systems:
1. Open system:
Exchanges both mass and energy with its surroundings.
Example: Turbine, compressor and boiler.
2. Closed system:
Exchanges energy but not mass with its surroundings.
Example: Gas in a sealed piston-cylinder arrangement.
3. Isolated system:
Exchanges neither mass nor energy with its surroundings.
Example: An ideal thermally insulated and sealed system.

THERMODYNAMIC PROPERTIES
Properties describe the state of a thermodynamic system.
 Intensive properties:
Independent of the mass of the system.
Examples:
- Temperature (T)
- Pressure (P)
- Density (ρ)
- Specific volume (v)
- Specific internal energy (u)
 Extensive properties:
Depend on the amount of matter.
Examples:
- Mass (m)
- Volume (V)
- Internal energy (U)
- Enthalpy (H)
- Entropy (S)
Specific properties are extensive properties per unit mass.
Specific volume:
    v = V/m
Density:
    ρ = m/V = 1/v

THERMODYNAMIC STATE AND EQUILIBRIUM
A thermodynamic state is defined by the values of the system's properties.
State variables include pressure, temperature and volume.
Thermodynamic equilibrium exists when a system has:
- Thermal equilibrium: Uniform temperature with no net heat flow.
- Mechanical equilibrium: No unbalanced pressure forces.
- Chemical equilibrium: No net chemical reaction or diffusion tendency.
- Phase equilibrium: No net change in the amounts of different phases.
A quasi-static process proceeds sufficiently slowly for the system to remain approximately in equilibrium throughout.

TEMPERATURE AND HEAT
Temperature measures the thermal state of a body and determines the direction of heat transfer.
Heat is energy transferred across a system boundary due to a temperature difference.
 Important distinction:
- Temperature is a property of a system.
- Heat is energy in transit, not a property stored in a system.
 Temperature conversions:
K = °C + 273.15
°F = (9/5)°C + 32
°C = (5/9)(°F - 32)
Absolute temperature must be expressed in kelvin in thermodynamic equations involving temperature ratios.

HEAT CAPACITY AND SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY
 Heat capacity:
The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a body by one kelvin.
    C = Q/ΔT
 Specific heat capacity:
The heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K.
    Q = mcΔT
Where:
Q = Heat transferred (J)
m = Mass (kg)
c = Specific heat capacity (J/kg·K)
ΔT = Temperature change (K)
 Molar heat capacity:
    Cₘ = Q/(nΔT)
Where n is the number of moles.
For an ideal gas:
    Cₚ - Cᵥ = R
    γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ
Where:
Cₚ = Specific heat capacity at constant pressure.
Cᵥ = Specific heat capacity at constant volume.
R = Specific gas constant.
γ = Heat capacity ratio.
For a monatomic ideal gas:
    Cᵥ = 3R/2
    Cₚ = 5R/2
    γ = 5/3
For a diatomic ideal gas at ordinary temperatures:
    Cᵥ ≈ 5R/2
    Cₚ ≈ 7R/2
    γ ≈ 1.4

THERMAL EXPANSION
Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
Linear expansion:
    ΔL = αL₀ΔT
    L = L₀(1 + αΔT)

Area expansion:
    ΔA = βA₀ΔT
 For isotropic solids:
    β ≈ 2α
Volume expansion:
    ΔV = γₜV₀ΔT
 For isotropic solids:
    γₜ ≈ 3α
Where:
α = Linear expansion coefficient (K⁻¹)
β = Area expansion coefficient (K⁻¹)
γₜ = Volume expansion coefficient (K⁻¹)
Applications:
- Expansion joints in bridges and railway tracks.
- Thermal stress analysis.
- Bimetallic thermostats.
- Design of pipelines and mechanical components.

THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFER
Heat is transferred through three main mechanisms.
 CONDUCTION
Transfer of thermal energy through a material without bulk movement of the material.
Fourier's law:
    Q̇ = -kA(dT/dx)
For a uniform plane wall:
    Q̇ = kA(T₁ - T₂)/L
Where:
Q̇ = Rate of heat transfer (W)
k = Thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
A = Cross-sectional area (m²)
L = Thickness (m)
The negative sign in Fourier's law indicates heat flows in the direction of decreasing temperature.
 CONVECTION
Heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid.
Newton's law of cooling:    
    Q̇ = hA(Tₛ - T∞)
Where:
h = Convective heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K)
Tₛ = Surface temperature
T∞ = Fluid temperature away from the surface.
 Types:
- Natural convection: Fluid motion caused by density differences.
- Forced convection: Fluid motion caused by fans, pumps or other external devices.
 RADIATION
Transfer of thermal energy through electromagnetic waves. It does not require a material medium.
Stefan-Boltzmann law:
    Q̇ = εσA(Tₛ⁴ - Tₛᵤᵣ⁴)
Where:
ε = Surface emissivity
σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²·K⁴
A = Surface area
Tₛ = Absolute surface temperature
Tₛᵤᵣ = Absolute surroundings temperature.
All temperatures must be in kelvin.

THERMODYNAMIC WORK
Work is energy transferred across a system boundary by means other than heat transfer.
For a moving piston:
    W = ∫P dV
For constant pressure:
    W = P(V₂ - V₁)
Sign convention commonly used:
- Expansion: Work done by the system is positive.
- Compression: Work done on the system is positive, so work by the system is negative.
Work is a path function, meaning it depends on the process followed.

INTERNAL ENERGY AND ENTHALPY
 Internal energy (U):
The total microscopic energy stored within a system due to molecular motion and interactions.
For a closed system:
    ΔU = m(u₂ - u₁)
For an ideal gas:
    ΔU = mcᵥ(T₂ - T₁)
 Enthalpy (H):
A thermodynamic property defined as:
    H = U + PV
Specific enthalpy:
    h = u + Pv
For an ideal gas:
    Δh = cₚ(T₂ - T₁)
Enthalpy is particularly useful in analysing open systems such as turbines, compressors and heat exchangers.

THE ZEROTH LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS
If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
 Importance:
- Establishes the concept of temperature.
- Forms the basis of temperature measurement.
- Explains how thermometers work.

FIRST LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS
The first law is the law of conservation of energy.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred or transformed.
For a closed system:
    Q - W = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE
Where:
Q = Heat supplied to the system.
W = Work done by the system.
ΔU = Change in internal energy.
ΔKE = Change in kinetic energy.
ΔPE = Change in potential energy.
If kinetic and potential energy changes are negligible:
    Q - W = ΔU
Or:
    Q = ΔU + W
For a cyclic process:
    ΔU = 0
Therefore:
    Qnet = Wnet
Applications:
- Piston-cylinder systems.
- Heat engines.
- Gas compression and expansion.
- Energy balance calculations.

THERMODYNAMIC PROCESSES
A thermodynamic process describes how a system changes from one state to another.
 ISOTHERMAL PROCESS
Temperature remains constant.
    T = constant
For an ideal gas:
    PV = constant
    ΔU = 0
Work done:
    W = mRT ln(V₂/V₁)
Therefore:
    Q = W
 ISOBARIC PROCESS
Pressure remains constant.
    P = constant
Work done:
    W = P(V₂ - V₁)
Heat transfer for an ideal gas:
    Q = mcₚ(T₂ - T₁)
 ISOCHORIC (ISOMETRIC) PROCESS
Volume remains constant.
    V = constant
    W = 0
Heat transfer:
    Q = ΔU
For an ideal gas:
    Q = mcᵥ(T₂ - T₁)
 ADIABATIC PROCESS
No heat transfer occurs between the system and surroundings.
    Q = 0
From the first law:
    ΔU = -W
For a reversible adiabatic ideal-gas process:
    PVᵞ = constant
    TVᵞ⁻¹ = constant    
    TᵞP¹⁻ᵞ = constant
 POLYTROPIC PROCESS
A process described by:
    PVⁿ = constant
Where n is the polytropic index.
Work done:
    W = (P₂V₂ - P₁V₁)/(1 - n)
For n ≠ 1.
For n = 1, the process becomes isothermal for an ideal gas.
 P-V AND T-S DIAGRAMS
P-V diagram:
Shows the relationship between pressure and volume during a thermodynamic process.
Important:
The area under a P-V curve represents boundary work.
T-S diagram:
Shows the relationship between temperature and entropy.
For an internally reversible process:
    Qrev = ∫T dS
The area under a T-S curve represents heat transfer for an internally reversible process.
Important process representations:
- Isothermal: Constant temperature.
- Isobaric: Constant pressure.
- Isochoric: Constant volume.
- Adiabatic: No heat transfer.
- Isentropic: Constant entropy.

SECOND LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS
The second law establishes the direction of natural thermodynamic processes and introduces entropy.
It explains why heat naturally flows from a hotter body to a colder body and why no heat engine can convert all supplied heat into useful work in a cycle.
 Kelvin-Planck statement:
No cyclic heat engine can convert all the heat absorbed from a single thermal reservoir into an equivalent amount of work.
 Clausius statement:
Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work.
Consequences:
- All real processes generate entropy.
- Heat engines have limited efficiency.
- Refrigerators require work input.
- Energy quality decreases during irreversible processes.

ENTROPY
Entropy (S) is a thermodynamic property that measures energy dispersal and the number of accessible microscopic configurations of a system.
For a reversible process:
    dS = δQrev/T
For a finite process:
    ΔS = ∫(δQrev/T)
For an ideal gas:
    Δs = cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + R ln(v₂/v₁)
Alternatively:
    Δs = cₚ ln(T₂/T₁) - R ln(P₂/P₁)
For an isolated system:
    ΔS_universe ≥ 0
- Reversible process: ΔS_universe = 0.
- Irreversible process: ΔS_universe > 0.
An isentropic process has:
    ΔS = 0
A reversible adiabatic process is isentropic.

HEAT ENGINES
A heat engine converts heat energy into mechanical work through a cyclic process.
It operates between:
- High-temperature reservoir (Tₕ).
- Low-temperature reservoir (T꜀).
Energy balance:
    Qₕ = W + Q꜀
Thermal efficiency:
    η = W/Qₕ
    η = 1 - Q꜀/Qₕ
Where:
Qₕ = Heat absorbed from hot reservoir.
Q꜀ = Heat rejected to cold reservoir.
W = Net work output.
No real heat engine can achieve 100% thermal efficiency.

CARNOT CYCLE
The Carnot cycle is an ideal reversible cycle operating between two thermal reservoirs.
It consists of four processes:
1. Reversible isothermal expansion.
2. Reversible adiabatic expansion.
3. Reversible isothermal compression.
4. Reversible adiabatic compression.
Carnot efficiency:
    η = 1 - T꜀/Tₕ
Temperatures must be in kelvin.
Importance:
- Establishes the maximum possible efficiency between two temperatures.
- Provides a benchmark for real heat engines.
- Depends only on reservoir temperatures.

REFRIGERATORS AND HEAT PUMPS
A refrigerator transfers heat from a low-temperature region to a high-temperature region using external work.
Coefficient of performance (COP):
    COPᵣ = Q꜀/W
Using energy balance:
    COPᵣ = Q꜀/(Qₕ - Q꜀)
For an ideal Carnot refrigerator:   
    COPᵣ = T꜀/(Tₕ - T꜀)
Heat pump:
Transfers heat into a region that needs heating.
    COPₕₚ = Qₕ/W
    COPₕₚ = Tₕ/(Tₕ - T꜀)  [Carnot]
Relationship:   
    COPₕₚ = COPᵣ + 1
 Applications:
- Refrigerators.
- Air conditioners.
- Heat pumps.
- Industrial cooling systems.

IDEAL GASES AND GAS LAWS
An ideal gas is a theoretical gas whose molecules have negligible volume and no intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions.
Ideal gas equation:
    PV = mRT
Or:
    PV = nRᵤT
Where:
P = Absolute pressure (Pa)
V = Volume (m³)
m = Mass (kg)
R = Specific gas constant (J/kg·K)
n = Number of moles
Rᵤ = Universal gas constant = 8.314 J/mol·K
T = Absolute temperature (K)
 Important gas laws:
Boyle's law:
At constant temperature:
    P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
Charles's law:
At constant pressure:
    V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
Pressure law:
At constant volume:
    P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂
Combined gas law:
    P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂
Always use absolute pressure and absolute temperature.

SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITIES OF GASES
At constant volume:
    Q = mcᵥΔT
At constant pressure:
    Q = mcₚΔT
For an ideal gas:   
    cₚ - cᵥ = R
    γ = cₚ/cᵥ
Constant-pressure heating requires more heat than constant-volume heating for the same temperature rise because the gas also performs expansion work.

PHASE CHANGES AND LATENT HEAT
Matter exists mainly in solid, liquid and gaseous states.
Phase changes include:
- Melting: Solid to liquid.
- Freezing: Liquid to solid.
- Vaporization: Liquid to gas.
- Condensation: Gas to liquid.
- Sublimation: Solid to gas.
- Deposition: Gas to solid.
During a phase change at constant pressure, temperature remains constant for a pure substance in equilibrium.
Latent heat:
    Q = mL
Where:
L = Specific latent heat (J/kg).
Sensible heat changes temperature without changing phase.
Latent heat changes phase without a temperature change during the idealized phase transition.

STEADY-FLOW ENERGY EQUATION
Used to analyse open systems in which fluid flows through control volumes.
General steady-flow energy equation:
    Q̇ - Ẇ =ṁ[(h₂ - h₁) + (V₂² - V₁²)/2 + g(z₂ - z₁)]
Where:
Q̇ = Heat transfer rate.
Ẇ = Work transfer rate.
ṁ = Mass flow rate.
h = Specific enthalpy.
V = Fluid velocity.
z = Elevation.
 Applications:
TURBINES
Convert fluid energy into mechanical work.
For an adiabatic turbine with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes:
    Ẇout = ṁ(h₁ - h₂)
COMPRESSORS
Increase fluid pressure using external work.
For an adiabatic compressor with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes:
    Ẇin = ṁ(h₂ - h₁)
NOZZLES
Convert pressure and enthalpy into kinetic energy.
For an adiabatic nozzle:
    h₁ + V₁²/2 = h₂ + V₂²/2
HEAT EXCHANGERS
Transfer thermal energy between fluids.
Energy lost by hot fluid = Energy gained by cold fluid, assuming negligible external heat losses and work.

THERMODYNAMIC CYCLES
A thermodynamic cycle is a series of processes after which the system returns to its initial state.
 Otto cycle:
Idealized cycle for spark-ignition petrol engines.
Processes:
- Isentropic compression.
- Constant-volume heat addition.
- Isentropic expansion.
- Constant-volume heat rejection.
Efficiency:
    η = 1 - 1/rᵞ⁻¹
 Diesel cycle:
Idealized cycle for compression-ignition diesel engines.
Processes:
- Isentropic compression.
- Constant-pressure heat addition.
- Isentropic expansion.
- Constant-volume heat rejection.
 Rankine cycle:
Idealized cycle for steam power plants.
Main components:
- Pump.
- Boiler.
- Turbine.
- Condenser.
 Brayton cycle:
Idealized cycle for gas turbines and jet engines.]
Processes:
- Isentropic compression.
- Constant-pressure heat addition.
- Isentropic expansion.
- Constant-pressure heat rejection.

THERMAL RESISTANCE
Thermal resistance measures opposition to heat flow.
For conduction through a plane wall:
    Rₜₕ = L/(kA)
For convection:
    Rₜₕ = 1/(hA)
Heat transfer rate:
    Q̇ = ΔT/Rₜₕ
For multiple layers in series:
    Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
This is analogous to electrical resistance in a circuit.
 Applications:
- Building insulation.
- Heat sinks.
- Electronic cooling.
- Thermal packaging.
- Industrial furnaces.

EXAMINATION TIPS AND IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS
1. Always distinguish heat, temperature, work and internal energy.
2. Heat and work are energy transfers, not thermodynamic properties.
3. Use kelvin for thermodynamic temperature ratios.
4. Distinguish between open, closed and isolated systems.
5. Identify the system boundary before applying energy equations.
6. Remember that internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature.
7. In an isothermal ideal-gas process, ΔU = 0.
8. In an adiabatic process, Q = 0, but temperature can change.
9. In an isochoric process, W = 0 because volume does not change.
10. In a cyclic process, the net change in internal energy is zero.
11. The area under a P-V diagram represents boundary work.
12. The area under a T-S diagram represents heat transfer for an internally reversible process.
13. Entropy of an isolated system never decreases.
14. Carnot efficiency is the maximum theoretical efficiency between two specified temperatures.
15. Refrigerator COP and heat-pump COP are not thermal efficiencies.
16. Use absolute pressure in ideal-gas calculations.
17. Distinguish specific heat capacity at constant pressure from that at constant volume.
18. In numerical problems, convert all quantities into consistent SI units.
19. Check whether a problem uses work done by the system or work done on the system.
20. For open-system problems, use the steady-flow energy equation rather than the closed-system equation.
