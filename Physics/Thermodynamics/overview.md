Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies how heat, energy, and work transform within physical systems.

    Overview and Core Laws
Thermodynamics tracks how energy moves and changes form while remaining conserved overall. It is built on four core laws:
* Zeroth Law: Establishes temperature and thermal equilibrium; if two systems are equal in temperature to a third, they are equal to each other.
* First Law: Conservation of energy; energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.
* Second Law: Entropy (disorder) always increases in a closed system, meaning heat flows naturally from hot to cold objects, and no energy conversion is 100% efficient.
* Third Law: A system's entropy approaches a minimum constant value as its temperature approaches absolute zero.

    Key Topics
* System & Surroundings: The focused area versus the rest of the universe, classified as open, closed, or isolated.
* State Variables & Properties: Measurable traits like pressure (P), volume (V), and temperature (T).
* Heat Transfer: The movement of thermal energy via conduction, convection, and radiation.
* Entropy (S) and Enthalpy (H): Measures of microscopic disorder and total heat content.

    Applications in Electronics and Computer Engineering
* Thermal Management & Cooling: CPUs and power electronics generate intense heat. Engineers use thermodynamic principles of conduction and convection to design heat sinks, cooling fans, and liquid cooling systems to prevent device failure.
* Joule Heating & Power Dissipation: Electric current passing through resistors and transistors generates unwanted thermal energy (Joule heating). Managing this heat is vital for maintaining safe operating temperatures in microchips.
* Energy Efficiency & Battery Life: Mobile devices and data centers rely on thermodynamic efficiency limits. Designing low-power VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) circuits minimizes energy waste and thermal degradation.
* Thermoelectric Devices: Technologies like Peltier coolers use thermoelectric effects (rooted in thermodynamics) for localized solid-state chip cooling.
