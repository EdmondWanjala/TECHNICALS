            FLUID MECHANICS
A fluid is a substance that continuously deforms (flows) when subjected to shear stress, regardless of how small the applied stress is.
 Types of fluids:
- Liquids: Have approximately constant volume but take the shape of their container.
- Gases: Have no fixed shape or volume and are compressible.
 Main branches:
- Fluid statics: Study of fluids at rest.
- Fluid dynamics: Study of fluids in motion.
- Fluid kinematics: Description of fluid motion without considering the forces causing it.
- Fluid kinetics: Study of fluid motion considering the forces involved.
 Applications:
- Hydraulic machines and braking systems.
- Water supply and sewage systems.
- Pumps, turbines and compressors.
- Aircraft aerodynamics.
- Ship and submarine design.
- Blood circulation and medical equipment.
- Industrial fluid transport and processing.

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES OF FLUIDS
 Density (ρ)
Mass per unit volume of a fluid.
    ρ = m/V
SI unit: kg/m³
Typical values:
- Water ≈ 1000 kg/m³
- Air ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ at standard room conditions.
 Specific weight (γ)
Weight per unit volume.
    γ = ρg
SI unit: N/m³
 Specific volume (v)
Volume occupied per unit mass.
    v = V/m = 1/ρ
SI unit: m³/kg
 Specific gravity (SG)
Ratio of the density of a substance to the density of a reference substance.
    SG = ρsubstance/ρwater
For liquids, water is commonly used as the reference.
SG has no unit.
 Viscosity
Resistance of a fluid to relative motion between adjacent layers.
Dynamic viscosity (μ):
    τ = μ(du/dy)
Where:
τ = shear stress (Pa)
μ = dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)
du/dy = velocity gradient perpendicular to flow.
Kinematic viscosity (ν):
    ν = μ/ρ
SI unit: m²/s
Important:
- High viscosity means greater resistance to flow.
- Honey has higher viscosity than water.
- Liquid viscosity generally decreases as temperature increases.
- Gas viscosity generally increases as temperature increases.
 Surface tension (σ)
Force per unit length acting along a liquid surface due to molecular attraction.
    σ = F/L
SI unit: N/m
It explains:
- Formation of spherical water droplets.
- Floating of small objects such as needles under suitable conditions.
- Capillary effects.
 Compressibility
The ability of a fluid to change volume under pressure.
Bulk modulus (K):
    K = -ΔP/(ΔV/V)
SI unit: Pa
A high bulk modulus indicates low compressibility.

FLUID PRESSURE
Pressure is the normal force exerted per unit area.
    P = F/A
SI unit: Pascal (Pa) = N/m²
Types of pressure:
 Atmospheric pressure
Pressure exerted by the surrounding atmosphere.
At sea level:
Patm ≈ 101325 Pa
 Absolute pressure
Pressure measured relative to a perfect vacuum.
 Gauge pressure
Pressure measured relative to atmospheric pressure.
    Pabsolute = Pgauge + Patm
 Vacuum pressure
Pressure below atmospheric pressure.
Hydrostatic pressure:
For a fluid at rest:
    P = ρgh
Where:
ρ = fluid density
g = gravitational acceleration
h = depth below the free surface.
Total absolute pressure:
    Pabsolute = Patm + ρgh
Important principles:
- Pressure increases with depth.
- Pressure at the same horizontal level in the same connected stationary fluid is equal.
- Pressure acts perpendicular to a surface.
- At a given depth, pressure depends on fluid density and gravity, not the container's shape.

PASCAL'S PRINCIPLE
Pascal's principle states that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished throughout the fluid and to the container walls.
    P1 = P2
    F1/A1 = F2/A2
Therefore:
    F2 = F1(A2/A1)
Applications:
- Hydraulic presses.
- Hydraulic lifts.
- Hydraulic brakes.
- Hydraulic jacks.
A small force applied to a small piston can produce a larger force on a larger piston.
Ideal hydraulic systems conserve work:
    F1d1 = F2d2

ARCHIMEDES' PRINCIPLE AND BUOYANCY
Archimedes' principle states that a body wholly or partially immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.
Buoyant force:
    FB = ρfluid g Vdisplaced
Where:
ρfluid = fluid density
Vdisplaced = volume of fluid displaced.
Apparent weight:
    Wapparent = Wactual - FB
Conditions:
- Floating: Buoyant force equals the object's weight.
- Sinking: Weight exceeds buoyant force.
- Rising: Buoyant force exceeds weight.
For a floating body:
    ρobject/ρfluid = Vsubmerged/Vobject
Applications:
- Ships and boats.
- Submarines.
- Hydrometers.
- Hot-air balloons.
- Density measurement.

FLUID FLOW
Fluid flow describes the movement of liquids and gases.
 Steady flow
Fluid properties at a given point do not change with time.
 Unsteady flow
Fluid properties at a point change with time.
 Laminar flow
Fluid moves in smooth, orderly layers with little mixing between layers.
 Turbulent flow
Fluid moves irregularly with fluctuations, eddies and mixing.
 Uniform flow
Velocity is constant from point to point at a given instant.
 Non-uniform flow
Velocity changes from point to point.

RATE OF FLOW AND DISCHARGE
Volume flow rate (Q):
    Q = V/t
For flow through a pipe:
    Q = Av
Where:
A = cross-sectional area
v = average fluid velocity.
SI unit: m³/s
Mass flow rate:
    ṁ = ρQ = ρAv
SI unit: kg/s
For a circular pipe:
    A = πD²/4
Therefore:
    Q = (πD²/4)v

EQUATION OF CONTINUITY
The continuity equation is based on conservation of mass.
For steady flow:
    Mass flow rate entering = Mass flow rate leaving.
General equation:
    ρ1A1v1 = ρ2A2v2
For an incompressible fluid (constant density):
    A1v1 = A2v2
Important:
- When pipe area decreases, flow velocity increases.
- When pipe area increases, flow velocity decreases.
Applications:
- Water pipelines.
- Nozzles and diffusers.
- Venturi meters.
- Blood flow through blood vessels.

BERNOULLI'S PRINCIPLE
Bernoulli's principle states that for steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow along a streamline, the total mechanical energy per unit volume remains constant.
    P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
Between two points:
    P1 + ½ρv1² + ρgh1= P2 + ½ρv2² + ρgh2
Where:
P = static pressure
½ρv² = dynamic pressure
ρgh = hydrostatic pressure contribution.
In head form:
    P/(ρg) + v²/(2g) + h = constant
Interpretation:
- An increase in fluid velocity is associated with a decrease in static pressure when elevation and other conditions are appropriately held constant.
- An increase in elevation reduces the pressure or velocity energy available if total energy is conserved.
Assumptions:
- Steady flow.
- Incompressible fluid.
- Negligible viscosity.
- Along a streamline.
Applications:
- Venturi meters.
- Pitot tubes.
- Atomisers and sprayers.
- Aircraft wing aerodynamics.
- Nozzles and flow measurement.

VENTURI EFFECT AND PITOT TUBE
 Venturi effect
When a fluid flows through a constricted section of a pipe, its velocity increases and its static pressure decreases under ideal Bernoulli conditions.
Venturi meter:
Used to measure flow rate through a pipe.
Combines:
- Continuity equation.
- Bernoulli's equation.
 Pitot tube
Measures fluid velocity by comparing stagnation pressure with static pressure.
For ideal incompressible flow:
    v = √[2(P0 - P)/ρ]
Where:
P0 = stagnation pressure
P = static pressure.

REYNOLDS NUMBER
Reynolds number predicts the flow regime by comparing inertial forces with viscous forces.
    Re = ρvD/μ
Or:
    Re = vD/ν
Where:
v = average flow velocity
D = pipe diameter
ρ = fluid density
μ = dynamic viscosity
ν = kinematic viscosity.
For flow in a circular pipe:
- Re < 2300: Generally laminar.
- 2300–4000: Transitional region.
- Re > 4000: Generally turbulent.
These are approximate ranges for fully developed pipe flow.
Importance:
- Predicts flow behaviour.
- Helps determine frictional losses.
- Used in pipe and hydraulic system design.

POISEUILLE'S LAW
Describes laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid through a long, straight, circular pipe under ideal conditions.
    Q = (πr⁴ΔP)/(8μL)
Where:
r = pipe radius
ΔP = pressure difference
μ = dynamic viscosity
L = pipe length.
Important:
- Flow rate increases with pressure difference.
- Flow rate increases with the fourth power of pipe radius.
- Flow rate decreases with viscosity and pipe length.
Applications:
- Blood flow analysis.
- Capillary flow.
- Microfluidic systems.

FLOW RESISTANCE AND PRESSURE LOSSES
Real fluids experience energy losses due to viscosity, friction and disturbances.
Darcy-Weisbach equation:
    hf = f(L/D)(v²/2g)
Where:
hf = head loss due to pipe friction
f = Darcy friction factor
L = pipe length
D = pipe diameter
v = average flow velocity.
Minor losses:
    hm = K(v²/2g)
Where:
    K = loss coefficient.
Minor losses occur due to:
- Bends and elbows.
- Valves.
- Pipe entrances and exits.
- Sudden expansions and contractions.
Total head loss:
    hL = hf + Σhm

HYDRAULIC POWER
Hydraulic power is the rate at which energy is transferred by a flowing fluid.
    Ph = ρgQH
Where:
ρ = fluid density
g = gravitational acceleration
Q = volume flow rate
H = hydraulic head.
For a pump:
    Pinput = ρgQH/η
Where:
    η = pump efficiency.
For a turbine:
    Poutput = ηρgQH
Applications:
- Water pumping.
- Hydroelectric power generation.
- Hydraulic machinery.

CAPILLARITY
Capillarity is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to surface tension and adhesive/cohesive forces.
Capillary rise:
    h = (2σ cos θ)/(ρgr)
Where:
σ = surface tension
θ = contact angle
ρ = liquid density
r = tube radius.
- Water generally rises in clean glass tubes.
- Mercury generally falls in glass tubes.
- Smaller tube radius produces a greater capillary effect.
Applications:
- Water movement in plants.
- Ink pens.
- Porous materials.
- Laboratory instruments.

IMPORTANT FLUID MECHANICS DIMENSIONLESS NUMBERS
Reynolds number:
    Re = ρvD/μ
Compares inertial and viscous forces.
Froude number:
    Fr = v/√(gL)
Compares inertial and gravitational effects.
Mach number:
    Ma = v/c
Compares flow velocity with the speed of sound.
Weber number:
    We = ρv²L/σ
Compares inertial and surface-tension effects.
These numbers are useful in fluid modelling, similarity analysis and engineering design.

IMPORTANT SI UNITS
- Density: kg/m³
- Pressure: Pa or N/m²
- Dynamic viscosity: Pa·s
- Kinematic viscosity: m²/s
- Surface tension: N/m
- Volume flow rate: m³/s
- Mass flow rate: kg/s
- Velocity: m/s
- Acceleration: m/s²
- Hydraulic power: W
- Head: m

EXAMINATION AND PROBLEM-SOLVING TIPS
1. Identify whether the problem involves fluid statics or fluid dynamics.
2. Write down all known quantities and convert them into SI units.
3. Draw a diagram showing pipe sections, elevations, pressure points or immersed bodies.
4. Identify assumptions: incompressible, steady, laminar or inviscid.
5. Choose the appropriate equation:
   - Pressure: P = F/A
   - Hydrostatics: P = ρgh
   - Buoyancy: FB = ρgV
   - Flow rate: Q = Av
   - Continuity: A1v1 = A2v2
   - Bernoulli: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
   - Reynolds number: Re = ρvD/μ
6. Substitute values carefully, maintaining consistent units.
7. Check whether the answer is physically reasonable.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Confusing mass density with specific weight.
- Using diameter instead of radius in area or Poiseuille calculations.
- Forgetting to convert mm to m.
- Confusing gauge pressure with absolute pressure.
- Applying Bernoulli's equation without checking its assumptions.
- Using the wrong Reynolds number flow regime.
- Confusing dynamic viscosity with kinematic viscosity.
- Omitting pressure, velocity or elevation terms in Bernoulli calculations.
