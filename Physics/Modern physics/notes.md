                MODERN PHYSICS
Modern physics is the branch of physics that explains physical phenomena that classical Newtonian mechanics and classical electromagnetic theory cannot adequately describe.
 It mainly covers:
- Quantum mechanics.
- Atomic physics.
- Nuclear physics.
- Relativity.
- Wave-particle duality.
- Quantum theory of radiation.
- Solid-state physics.
- Semiconductor physics.
- Particle physics.
Modern physics is essential in electronics, computing, telecommunications, nuclear energy, medical imaging, nanotechnology and advanced engineering.

FAILURE OF CLASSICAL PHYSICS
Classical physics successfully explains macroscopic objects moving at ordinary speeds but fails to adequately explain certain microscopic and high-energy phenomena.
Important limitations:
 Blackbody radiation:
Classical theory predicted infinite energy at high frequencies, known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.
Planck resolved this by proposing that energy is emitted or absorbed in discrete packets called quanta.
 Photoelectric effect:
Classical wave theory could not explain why electrons are emitted only above a certain frequency, regardless of light intensity.
Einstein explained this using photons.
 Atomic stability:
Classical electrodynamics predicted that orbiting electrons should continuously radiate energy and collapse into the nucleus.
Quantum theory explains the existence of stable atomic energy states.
 Atomic spectra:
Classical physics could not explain the discrete spectral lines produced by atoms.
Quantum theory explains them through transitions between quantized energy levels.

QUANTUM THEORY AND PLANCK'S HYPOTHESIS
Quantum theory states that energy exchange at the microscopic level occurs in discrete amounts rather than arbitrary continuous values.
Planck proposed that the energy of an oscillator is quantized:
    E = nhf
Where:
E = Energy
n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
h = Planck's constant
f = Frequency
Energy of one quantum:
    E = hf
Planck's constant:
    h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
Angular-frequency form:
    E = ℏω
Where:
    ℏ = h/2π
    ω = 2πf
Applications:
- Quantum electronics.
- Lasers.
- Semiconductor devices.
- Atomic spectroscopy.

PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT
The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material surface when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency strikes it.
The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.
Einstein's photoelectric equation:
    hf = φ + Kmax
Therefore:
    Kmax = hf - φ
Also:
    Kmax = eVs
Hence:
    eVs = hf - φ
Where:
h = Planck's constant
f = Frequency of incident light
φ = Work function of the material
Kmax = Maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons
e = Electron charge
Vs = Stopping potential
Threshold frequency:
    f₀ = φ/h
Threshold wavelength:
    λ₀ = hc/φ
Important observations:
- Electrons are emitted only when frequency exceeds the threshold frequency.
- Increasing light intensity increases the number of emitted electrons, provided the frequency is above threshold.
- Increasing frequency increases the maximum kinetic energy.
- Photoelectric emission occurs with negligible delay under suitable illumination.
 Applications:
- Photocells.
- Light sensors.
- Automatic lighting systems.
- Solar cells (related photovoltaic phenomenon).
- Optical detection systems.

WAVE-PARTICLE DUALITY
Wave-particle duality states that matter and electromagnetic radiation exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties.
 Light:
- Wave nature: Interference, diffraction and polarization.
- Particle nature: Photoelectric effect and photon interactions.
 Matter:
- Particle nature: Electrons and other particles possess mass and momentum.
- Wave nature: Electrons exhibit diffraction and interference.
This principle is fundamental to quantum mechanics.

PHOTONS AND ELECTROMAGNETIC RADIATION
A photon is a discrete packet (quantum) of electromagnetic radiation.
Energy of a photon:
    E = hf
Since:
    c = fλ
Therefore:
    E = hc/λ
Photon momentum:
    p = h/λ
Also:
    p = E/c
Properties:
- Photons have zero rest mass.
- They travel at the speed of light in vacuum.
- They carry energy and momentum.
- Their energy depends on frequency, not intensity.
  Applications:
- Lasers.
- Optical communication.
- Solar energy conversion.
- Photodetectors.
- Medical imaging.

COMPTON EFFECT
The Compton effect is the increase in wavelength of an X-ray or gamma-ray photon after scattering from an electron.
It demonstrates the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation and conservation of energy and momentum.
Compton wavelength shift:
    Δλ = λ' - λ
    Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 - cos θ)
Where:
λ = Initial photon wavelength
λ' = Scattered photon wavelength
mₑ = Electron rest mass
θ = Photon scattering angle
Compton wavelength of an electron:
    λc = h/mₑc
The wavelength shift depends on the scattering angle.
 Applications:
- X-ray scattering analysis.
- Medical physics.
- Material analysis.
- Radiation detection.

DE BROGLIE MATTER WAVES
Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving material particle has an associated wavelength.
De Broglie wavelength:
    λ = h/p
For a non-relativistic particle:
    λ = h/mv
Where:
h = Planck's constant
p = Momentum
m = Particle mass
v = Particle velocity
For an electron accelerated through potential difference V:
    eV = 1/2 mv²
Therefore:
    λ = h/√(2meV)
This expression assumes non-relativistic electron speeds.
 Experimental evidence:
Electron diffraction demonstrates the wave nature of matter.
 Applications:
- Electron microscopes.
- Quantum electronics.
- Electron diffraction.
- Nanotechnology.

HEISENBERG UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE
The uncertainty principle states that certain pairs of physical quantities cannot simultaneously be known with arbitrary precision.
Position-momentum relationship:
    ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2
Energy-time relationship:
    ΔEΔt ≥ ℏ/2
Where:
Δx = Uncertainty in position
Δp = Uncertainty in momentum
ΔE = Uncertainty in energy
Δt = Characteristic measurement time interval
ℏ = Reduced Planck's constant
 Importance:
- Explains limitations in microscopic measurements.
- Forms a foundation of quantum mechanics.
- Explains why electrons do not have definite classical orbits.
- Important in atomic and subatomic physics.

BOHR MODEL OF THE ATOM
Niels Bohr proposed a model of the hydrogen atom based on quantized electron orbits.
 Main postulates:
1. Electrons occupy certain permitted stationary energy states.
2. Electrons do not continuously radiate energy while in these stationary states.
3. Radiation is absorbed or emitted when an electron transitions between energy levels.
Angular momentum quantization:
    mvr = nℏ
Radius of the nth orbit in hydrogen:
    rₙ = n²a₀
Where:
    a₀ = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m
Energy levels:
    Eₙ = -13.6/n² eV
For hydrogen, where n = 1, 2, 3, ...
Ground state energy:
    E₁ = -13.6 eV
Excited states have higher (less negative) energy.
Photon energy during a transition:
    hf = |E₂ - E₁|
Important:
- Absorption occurs when an electron moves to a higher energy level.
- Emission occurs when an electron moves to a lower energy level.
Limitations:
- Works well for hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions.
- Does not accurately explain complex multi-electron atoms.
- Does not fully explain spectral line intensities and fine structure.

ATOMIC SPECTRA
Atoms absorb or emit radiation at specific frequencies corresponding to differences between allowed energy levels.
-Energy transition:
    ΔE = hf = hc/λ
-Emission spectrum:
Produced when excited electrons move to lower energy states.
-Absorption spectrum:
Produced when electrons absorb photons and move to higher energy states.
-Hydrogen spectral series:
 Lyman series:
Transitions ending at n = 1.
Occurs mainly in the ultraviolet region.
 Balmer series:
Transitions ending at n = 2.
Contains important visible spectral lines.
 Paschen series:
Transitions ending at n = 3.
Occurs in the infrared region.
Rydberg equation:
    1/λ = Rₕ(1/n₁² - 1/n₂²)
Where:
Rₕ = Rydberg constant ≈ 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹
n₂ > n₁
Applications:
- Chemical element identification.
- Astronomy.
- Spectroscopy.
- Plasma analysis.

X-RAYS
X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves with short wavelengths.
They are commonly produced when high-speed electrons strike a metal target.
Two main production mechanisms:
 Bremsstrahlung radiation:
Produced when high-speed electrons are decelerated by the electric fields of target nuclei.
 Characteristic X-rays:
Produced when inner-shell atomic electrons are removed and outer-shell electrons transition into the vacancies.
Minimum X-ray wavelength:
    λmin = hc/eV
Where V is the accelerating potential difference.
Properties:
- High penetrating power.
- Ionizing radiation.
- Can cause fluorescence.
- Can expose photographic materials.
- Can be diffracted by crystal structures.
Applications:
- Medical radiography.
- CT scanning.
- Industrial inspection.
- X-ray crystallography.
- Security scanning.

NUCLEAR PHYSICS
Nuclear physics studies atomic nuclei, their structure, properties and interactions.
An atomic nucleus consists of:
- Protons: Positively charged particles.
- Neutrons: Electrically neutral particles.
Atomic number (Z):
Number of protons in the nucleus.
Mass number (A):
Total number of protons and neutrons.
    A = Z + N
Where N is the neutron number.
Notation:
    ᴬ_ZX
Where X represents the chemical element.
Isotopes:
Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different numbers of neutrons.
Isobars:
Atoms with the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
Isotones:
Nuclei with the same number of neutrons but different atomic numbers.

MASS DEFECT AND BINDING ENERGY
The mass of a stable nucleus is generally less than the total mass of its separate constituent nucleons.
This difference is called mass defect.
Mass defect:
    Δm = Zmₚ + Nmₙ - M_nucleus
Nuclear binding energy:
    E_b = Δmc²
Where:
mₚ = Proton mass
mₙ = Neutron mass
M_nucleus = Actual nuclear mass
c = Speed of light.
Using atomic masses:
    Δm = Zm_H + Nmₙ - M_atom
Where m_H is the mass of a neutral hydrogen atom.
Binding energy per nucleon:
    E_b/A
Importance:
- Measures nuclear stability.
- Explains energy release in nuclear reactions.
- Helps explain nuclear fusion and fission.
A higher binding energy per nucleon generally indicates greater nuclear stability.

RADIOACTIVITY
Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of radiation.
Three major types:
 ALPHA (α) DECAY
An alpha particle consists of two protons and two neutrons.
It is equivalent to a helium-4 nucleus.
Nuclear equation:
    ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ⁻⁴_Z⁻²Y + ⁴₂He
Properties:
- Strong ionizing power.
- Low penetrating power.
- Stopped by paper or the outer layer of skin.
 BETA (β) DECAY
Beta-minus decay occurs when a neutron changes into a proton.
    n → p + e⁻ + anti-νₑ
Nuclear equation:
    ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ_Z+1Y + e⁻ + anti-νₑ
Beta-plus decay occurs when a proton changes into a neutron.
    p → n + e⁺ + νₑ
Beta radiation has moderate penetrating and ionizing power compared with alpha radiation.
 GAMMA (γ) DECAY
Gamma radiation consists of high-energy electromagnetic photons emitted by an excited nucleus.
    ᴬ_ZX* → ᴬ_ZX + γ
Properties:
- No mass or electric charge.
- High penetrating power.
- Relatively low ionizing power compared with alpha particles.
- Requires thick lead or concrete shielding, depending on energy and intensity.

RADIOACTIVE DECAY LAW
Radioactive decay is a random process in which the probability of decay per nucleus per unit time is constant.
Number of undecayed nuclei:
    N = N₀e⁻λt
Activity:
    A = λN
    A = A₀e⁻λt
Where:
N₀ = Initial number of nuclei
N = Number remaining after time t
λ = Decay constant
A₀ = Initial activity
A = Activity at time t
Half-life:
    T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ
Mean lifetime:
    τ = 1/λ
Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq).
    1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
Applications:
- Medical diagnosis and treatment.
- Radiometric dating.
- Industrial thickness measurement.
- Nuclear power.
- Tracer studies.

NUCLEAR FISSION
Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into two or more lighter nuclei, usually accompanied by the release of neutrons and energy.
Example:
Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and undergoes fission.
Characteristics:
- Releases a large amount of energy.
- Produces additional neutrons.
- Can initiate a chain reaction.
A controlled chain reaction is used in nuclear reactors.
An uncontrolled chain reaction can occur in nuclear weapons.
Applications:
- Nuclear power generation.
- Research reactors.
- Production of radioisotopes.

NUCLEAR FUSION
Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy.
Example:
    ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + energy
Fusion requires extremely high temperatures to overcome electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei.
Applications:
- Energy production research.
- Stellar energy generation.
- Study of plasma physics.
The Sun produces energy primarily through nuclear fusion.

MASS-ENERGY EQUIVALENCE
Einstein established that mass and energy are equivalent.
    E = mc²
Where:
E = Energy (J)
m = Mass (kg)
c = Speed of light.
For a change in mass:
    ΔE = Δmc²
A small mass difference corresponds to a very large amount of energy because c² is extremely large.
Applications:
- Nuclear reactors.
- Nuclear weapons.
- Particle physics.
- Nuclear medicine.
- Astrophysics.

SPECIAL THEORY OF RELATIVITY
Einstein's special theory of relativity describes physical laws for observers in inertial reference frames, particularly at speeds approaching the speed of light.
Two postulates:
1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames.
2. The speed of light in vacuum is constant for all inertial observers, independent of the motion of the source or observer.
Lorentz factor:
    γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²)
 TIME DILATION
A moving clock is measured to run slower relative to an inertial observer for whom the clock is moving.
    Δt = γΔt₀
Where Δt₀ is the proper time.
 LENGTH CONTRACTION
An object's length along its direction of motion is measured to be shorter by an observer relative to its proper length.
    L = L₀/γ
 RELATIVISTIC MOMENTUM
    p = γmv
 RELATIVISTIC ENERGY
    E = γmc²
Rest energy:
    E₀ = mc²
Kinetic energy:
    K = (γ - 1)mc²
Mass-energy relationship:   
    E² = (pc)² + (mc²)²
Relativistic effects become significant when velocity approaches the speed of light.

QUANTUM MECHANICS
Quantum mechanics is the theory describing the behaviour of matter and radiation at atomic and subatomic scales.
Important principles:
- Wave-particle duality.
- Energy quantization.
- Superposition.
- Probability-based measurement.
- Uncertainty principle.
Wave function:
    ψ(x,t)
The wave function describes the quantum state of a system.
Born interpretation:
    |ψ(x,t)|²
Represents probability density of finding a particle at position x and time t.
Normalization condition:
    ∫|ψ|² dV = 1
Time-dependent Schrödinger equation:
    iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ
Where:
i = Imaginary unit
ℏ = Reduced Planck's constant
Ĥ = Hamiltonian operator
ψ = Wave function.
For a one-dimensional particle:
    iℏ ∂ψ/∂t =- (ℏ²/2m)(∂²ψ/∂x²) + Vψ
Time-independent Schrödinger equation:
    Ĥψ = Eψ
Quantum mechanics is fundamental to semiconductors, transistors, lasers and quantum computing.

QUANTUM TUNNELLING
Quantum tunnelling occurs when a particle passes through a potential energy barrier even though its energy is less than the barrier height.
This is possible because the particle is described by a wave function that can extend into and beyond the barrier.
 Applications:
- Tunnel diodes.
- Scanning tunnelling microscopes.
- Nuclear fusion.
- Flash memory devices.
- Quantum electronics.

SOLID-STATE PHYSICS AND ENERGY BANDS
Solid-state physics studies the physical properties of solids, particularly the behaviour of electrons in crystalline materials.
In solids, closely spaced atomic energy levels form energy bands.
Important bands:
 Valence band:
The highest energy band normally occupied by electrons at absolute zero.
 Conduction band:
The energy band in which electrons can move through the material and contribute to electrical conduction.
 Forbidden energy gap:
The energy region between the valence and conduction bands where no allowed electronic states exist in an ideal crystal.
Classification:
Conductors:
- Valence and conduction bands overlap or a band is partially filled.
- High electrical conductivity.
Semiconductors:
- Have a relatively small band gap.
- Conductivity can be controlled by temperature, doping and applied fields.
Insulators:
- Have a large band gap.
- Very low electrical conductivity under ordinary conditions.

SEMICONDUCTOR PHYSICS
A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators and can be controlled.
Common materials:
- Silicon (Si).
- Germanium (Ge).
- Gallium arsenide (GaAs).
 INTRINSIC SEMICONDUCTORS
Pure semiconductors without intentional impurity doping.
Electrical conduction occurs through:
- Electrons.
- Holes.
Electron-hole pairs are generated when electrons gain sufficient energy to move into the conduction band.
 EXTRINSIC SEMICONDUCTORS
Semiconductors whose electrical properties are modified by adding impurities (doping).
N-type:
Doped with donor impurities that contribute extra electrons.
P-type:
Doped with acceptor impurities that create holes.
Majority carriers:
- N-type: Electrons.
- P-type: Holes.
Minority carriers:
- N-type: Holes.
- P-type: Electrons.
Applications:
- Diodes.
- Transistors.
- Integrated circuits.
- Solar cells.
- LEDs.
- Sensors.

P-N JUNCTION
A P-N junction is formed by joining p-type and n-type semiconductor regions.
When the junction is formed:
- Electrons diffuse from the n-region to the p-region.
- Holes diffuse from the p-region to the n-region.
- Recombination occurs near the junction.
- A depletion region forms.
- An internal electric field and potential barrier develop.
Forward bias:
- P-side connected to positive terminal.
- N-side connected to negative terminal.
- Barrier potential decreases.
- Current flows readily after sufficient forward bias.
Reverse bias:
- P-side connected to negative terminal.
- N-side connected to positive terminal.
- Depletion region widens.
- Only a small reverse leakage current normally flows until breakdown.
Applications:
- Rectification.
- Switching.
- Signal detection.
- Voltage regulation.
- Electronic protection.

EXAMINATION TIPS AND IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS
1. Distinguish between classical physics and modern physics.
2. Remember that photon energy depends on frequency, not intensity.
3. In the photoelectric effect, intensity affects the number of emitted electrons, while frequency determines their maximum kinetic energy.
4. Distinguish between photon momentum and classical particle momentum.
5. Use λ = h/mv for non-relativistic de Broglie calculations.
6. Remember that electron energy levels in hydrogen are negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus.
7. In atomic transitions, emitted photon energy equals the energy difference between levels.
8. Distinguish between isotopes, isobars and isotones.
9. In nuclear equations, conserve both mass number and atomic number.
10. Remember that alpha decay reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.
11. Beta-minus decay increases atomic number by 1 without changing mass number.
12. Gamma emission does not change mass number or atomic number.
13. Use N = N₀e⁻λt for radioactive decay.
14. Convert mass defect into energy using E = Δmc².
15. Distinguish nuclear fission (splitting) from fusion (joining).
16. Time dilation and length contraction become significant near the speed of light.
17. Understand that a wave function is a probability amplitude, not a direct physical probability.
18. Distinguish conductors, semiconductors and insulators using energy-band theory.
19. Understand majority and minority charge carriers in P-type and N-type materials.
20. In semiconductor junction problems, identify the biasing polarity before predicting current flow.
21. Convert electron volts to joules when SI energy units are required.
22. Always check units, signs and conservation laws in numerical problems.
