            ELEMENTARY PHYSICS
Physics is the branch of science concerned with matter, energy, motion, forces, space and time, and their interactions.
  Importance in engineering:
- Provides principles used in designing machines, structures and electrical systems.
- Explains the operation of motors, generators, sensors and electronic devices.
- Supports analysis of heat transfer, fluid flow and mechanical systems.
- Helps predict and control the behaviour of physical systems.
  Main branches:
- Mechanics: motion, forces and energy.
- Thermodynamics: heat, temperature and energy transfer.
- Electromagnetism: electric and magnetic phenomena.
- Optics: light and its behaviour.
- Waves and acoustics: oscillations, sound and wave propagation.
- Modern physics: atomic, nuclear and quantum phenomena.

PHYSICAL QUANTITIES AND MEASUREMENTS
A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as a numerical value and a unit.
  Types:
Fundamental quantities:
- Length (metre, m)
- Mass (kilogram, kg)
- Time (second, s)
- Electric current (ampere, A)
- Temperature (kelvin, K)
- Amount of substance (mole, mol)
- Luminous intensity (candela, cd)
Derived quantities:
Obtained by combining fundamental quantities.
Examples:
- Area: m²
- Volume: m³
- Velocity: m/s
- Acceleration: m/s²
- Force: newton (N)
- Energy: joule (J)
- Pressure: pascal (Pa)
Dimensional analysis:
Used to check the consistency of physical equations.
Examples:
- Velocity = [LT⁻¹]
- Acceleration = [LT⁻²]
- Force = [MLT⁻²]
- Energy = [ML²T⁻²]
- Pressure = [ML⁻¹T⁻²]
Measurement concepts:
- Accuracy: closeness to the true value.
- Precision: closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
- Resolution: smallest change an instrument can detect.
- Systematic error: consistent error caused by calibration or method.
- Random error: unpredictable variation in measurements.
Percentage error:
    Percentage error = (Absolute error / True value) × 100%
Significant figures:
- Indicate the precision of a measured value.
- Final answers should reflect the precision of the given data.

SCALARS AND VECTORS
Scalar quantities have magnitude only.
Examples: mass, time, temperature, energy and speed.
Vector quantities have both magnitude and direction.
Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and momentum.
Vector components:
For a vector A at angle θ to the positive x-axis:
    Ax = A cos θ
    Ay = A sin θ
Magnitude:
    A = √(Ax² + Ay²)
Direction:
    θ = tan⁻¹(Ay/Ax)
Vector addition:
Resolve vectors into components, add corresponding components, then calculate the resultant.
Resultant:
    Rx = ΣAx    
    Ry = ΣAy
    R = √(Rx² + Ry²)

MATTER AND ITS PROPERTIES
Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.
States of matter:
- Solid: definite shape and volume.
- Liquid: definite volume but takes the shape of its container.
- Gas: no definite shape or volume.
- Plasma: ionised gas containing charged particles.
Basic properties:
- Mass: quantity of matter in a body.
- Volume: space occupied by a body.
- Density: mass per unit volume.
- Weight: gravitational force acting on a body.
Density:
    ρ = m/V
Relative density:
    Relative density = Density of substance / Density of water
Pressure:
    P = F/A
Hydrostatic pressure:
    P = ρgh
Where:
ρ = fluid density
g = gravitational acceleration
h = depth

MOTION (BASIC KINEMATICS)
Motion is the change in position of an object with time.
Important terms:
- Distance: total path travelled; scalar.
- Displacement: change in position; vector.
- Speed: distance travelled per unit time.
- Velocity: displacement per unit time.
- Acceleration: rate of change of velocity.
    Average speed = Total distance / Total time
    Average velocity = Displacement / Time
Acceleration:
    a = (v - u)/t
Equations of uniformly accelerated motion:
v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s = ½(u + v)t
Where:
u = initial velocity
v = final velocity
a = acceleration
s = displacement
t = time
Free fall:
Objects falling under gravity experience acceleration g ≈ 9.81 m/s², neglecting air resistance.

FORCE AND NEWTON'S LAWS
Force is an interaction that changes or tends to change the motion or shape of a body.
Newton's First Law:
A body remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted upon by a resultant external force.
This is the law of inertia.
Newton's Second Law:
The resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum.
For constant mass:
    F = ma
Newton's Third Law:
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. The forces act on different bodies.
Weight:
    W = mg
Friction:
A force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of motion between surfaces.
Limiting friction:
    Fmax = μN
Where:
μ = coefficient of friction
N = normal reaction
Momentum:
    p = mv
Impulse:
    J = FΔt = Δp
Conservation of momentum:
Total momentum before collision = Total momentum after collision, provided the system has no net external force.

WORK, ENERGY AND POWER
Work is done when a force causes displacement.
    W = Fs cos θ
Kinetic energy:
    KE = ½mv²
Gravitational potential energy:
    PE = mgh
Elastic potential energy:
    E = ½kx²
Power:
    P = W/t
For constant force and velocity:
    P = Fv
Efficiency:
    Efficiency = (Useful output energy / Total input energy) × 100%
Law of conservation of energy:
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred or transformed.

SIMPLE MACHINES
A machine makes work easier by changing the magnitude or direction of a force.
Examples:
- Lever
- Pulley
- Inclined plane
- Wheel and axle
- Screw
- Gear system
Mechanical advantage:
    MA = Load / Effort
Velocity ratio:
    VR = Distance moved by effort / Distance moved by load
Efficiency:
    η = (MA/VR) × 100%
An ideal machine has 100% efficiency. Real machines have losses due to friction and other effects.

HEAT AND TEMPERATURE
Temperature measures the degree of hotness or coldness of a body and relates to the average kinetic energy of its particles.
Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
Temperature conversions:
    K = °C + 273.15
    °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32
Specific heat capacity:
    Q = mcΔT
Where:
Q = heat transferred
m = mass
c = specific heat capacity
ΔT = temperature change
Latent heat:
    Q = mL
Heat transfer methods:
- Conduction: transfer through direct particle interaction.
- Convection: transfer through bulk movement of fluids.
- Radiation: transfer through electromagnetic waves.

WAVES AND SOUND
A wave transfers energy from one point to another without net transfer of matter.
Types:
- Transverse waves: oscillations perpendicular to direction of propagation.
- Longitudinal waves: oscillations parallel to direction of propagation.
Wave equation:
    v = fλ
Where:
v = wave speed
f = frequency
λ = wavelength
Period:
    T = 1/f
Sound:
- Requires a material medium.
- Travels through solids, liquids and gases.
- Cannot travel through a vacuum.
- Frequency determines pitch.
- Amplitude is related to loudness.

BASIC ELECTRICITY
Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.
Types of charge:
- Positive
- Negative
Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
Electric current:
    I = Q/t
Potential difference:
    V = W/Q
Resistance:
    R = V/I
Ohm's Law:
    V = IR
Electrical power:
    P = VI = I²R = V²/R
Electrical energy:
    E = Pt
Circuit arrangements:
Series:
- Same current through all components.
- Total resistance: RT = R1 + R2 + ...
- Supply voltage is shared.
Parallel:
- Same voltage across each branch.
- Total resistance: 1/RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...
- Current divides between branches.

BASIC MAGNETISM
Magnetism is associated with moving electric charges and magnetic dipoles.
Properties:
- Like magnetic poles repel.
- Unlike poles attract.
- Magnetic field lines travel from north to south outside a magnet.
- Magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.
Force on a current-carrying conductor:
    F = BIL sin θ
Where:
B = magnetic flux density
I = current
L = conductor length in the field
θ = angle between conductor and magnetic field
Electromagnetic induction:
A changing magnetic flux through a coil induces an electromotive force.
Faraday's law:
    ε = -N(dΦ/dt)
Applications:
- Electric generators
- Transformers
- Induction motors
- Wireless charging

LIGHT AND BASIC OPTICS
Light is electromagnetic radiation that can travel through a vacuum.
Reflection:
Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection.
Refraction:
Change in direction of light when it passes between media because its speed changes.
Snell's law:
    n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
Refractive index:
    n = c/v
Lens formula (using the Cartesian sign convention):
    1/f = 1/v - 1/u
Magnification:
    m = image height/object height = v/u
Applications:
- Cameras
- Microscopes
- Telescopes
- Optical fibre communication
- Sensors

ATOMIC AND NUCLEAR BASICS
An atom consists of:
- Protons: positively charged.
- Neutrons: electrically neutral.
- Electrons: negatively charged.
Atomic number (Z):
Number of protons.
Mass number (A):
Total number of protons and neutrons.
    A = Z + N
Isotopes:
Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
Radioactivity:
Spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei.
Types of radiation:
- Alpha (α): helium nuclei; high ionisation, low penetration.
- Beta (β): electrons or positrons; moderate penetration.
- Gamma (γ): electromagnetic radiation; high penetration.
Radioactive decay:
    N = N₀e^(-λt)
Half-life:
    T½ = ln(2)/λ
Mass-energy equivalence:
    E = mc²

EXAMINATION AND PROBLEM-SOLVING TIPS
1. Identify the physical quantity being calculated.
2. Write down the given data and convert all values to SI units.
3. Draw a diagram where forces, vectors, circuits or wave directions are involved.
4. Select the appropriate physical law or equation.
5. Rearrange the equation before substituting values.
6. Substitute values carefully, including units.
7. Check dimensions and the reasonableness of the result.
8. State the final answer with the correct SI unit.

Common mistakes:
- Confusing mass (kg) with weight (N).
- Confusing distance with displacement.
- Confusing speed with velocity.
- Using Celsius instead of kelvin in thermodynamic equations where absolute temperature is required.
- Ignoring vector directions and signs.
- Mixing units without conversion.
- Omitting units from final answers.
