                ENGINEERING DRAWING
Engineering drawing is the graphical language used to communicate the shape, size, construction, and specifications of engineering objects using standardized lines, symbols, dimensions, and projections.
Importance;
- Communicates technical ideas accurately between engineers, designers, and manufacturers.
- Provides information for manufacturing, construction, assembly, and inspection.
- Specifies dimensions, tolerances, materials, and surface finishes.
- Reduces ambiguity and manufacturing errors.
- Supports CAD (Computer-Aided Design), product development, and technical documentation.
Types of Engineering Drawing;
1. Mechanical drawing: Machine components, gears, shafts, and assemblies.
2. Civil/structural drawing: Buildings, bridges, foundations, and structural members.
3. Electrical drawing: Wiring diagrams, circuit layouts, and installation plans.
4. Architectural drawing: Building plans, elevations, and sections.
5. Piping drawing: Pipe routes, fittings, and process systems.
6. Assembly drawing: Shows how components fit together.
7. Detail drawing: Gives complete manufacturing information for an individual component.

DRAWING INSTRUMENTS AND EQUIPMENT:
- Drawing board: Provides a flat drawing surface.
- T-square or parallel rule: Draws horizontal and parallel lines.
- Set squares: Draw standard angles such as 30°, 45°, 60°, and 90°.
- Compass: Draws circles and arcs.
- Divider: Transfers distances and divides lines.
- Protractor: Measures and constructs angles.
- Scale ruler: Measures or constructs scaled drawings.
- French curves: Draws smooth irregular curves.
- Pencils: H and 2H for thin construction lines; HB for general work and lettering, depending on the required line weight.
- Eraser and erasing shield: Correct unwanted marks precisely.
Modern engineering drawing also uses CAD software such as AutoCAD, SolidWorks, Fusion, and FreeCAD.

DRAWING SHEETS, BORDERS AND TITLE BLOCKS:
A drawing sheet normally contains;
- Border: Defines the drawing area.
- Title block: Contains the drawing title, drawing number, scale, units, date, author, and approval details.
- Revision information: Records changes made to the drawing.
- Projection symbol: Identifies the projection method where required.
- Notes and material specifications: Provide additional technical requirements.
Common ISO A-series paper sizes;
Sheet| Dimensions (mm)
A0| 841 × 1189
A1| 594 × 841
A2| 420 × 594
A3| 297 × 420
A4| 210 × 297
Each successive size has half the area of the previous larger size.

TYPES OF LINES AND THEIR USES:
Correct line types distinguish visible features, hidden features, dimensions, and reference information.
- Continuous thick line: Visible outlines and edges.
- Continuous thin line: Dimension lines, extension lines, projection lines, leaders, and hatching.
- Dashed thin line: Hidden edges and features.
- Chain thin line (long dash–short dash): Centre lines, axes, and lines of symmetry.
- Cutting-plane line: Indicates where an object is imagined to be cut; its prescribed line pattern and thickness depend on the drafting standard.
- Freehand or zigzag break line: Indicates that part of an object has been omitted.
- Phantom chain line: May show alternate positions, adjacent parts, or motion-related information.
Rules;
- Use consistent line weights and patterns.
- Do not confuse hidden lines with centre lines.
- Where lines overlap, follow the applicable drafting standard for precedence.
- Make visible outlines clear and prominent.

LETTERING AND ANNOTATION:
Engineering lettering must be clear, uniform, legible, and suitable for reproduction.
Important features:
- Use consistent letter height and spacing.
- Use upright or inclined lettering according to the drawing standard.
- Write dimensions and notes clearly.
- Use standard symbols and abbreviations where recognized.
Examples:
- Ø = diameter
- R = radius
- □ = square section
- ± = plus or minus
- ° = degrees
- M = metric screw thread designation when followed by the appropriate thread size, e.g. M10.

SCALES IN ENGINEERING DRAWING:
A scale represents the relationship between the dimensions on a drawing and the actual dimensions of an object.
Scale ratio;
    Scale = Drawing length / Actual length
Both lengths must be expressed in the same units.
Types of scales;
1. Full-size scale: 1:1
2. Reducing scale: 1:n, where n > 1; e.g. 1:50.
3. Enlarging scale: n:1, where n > 1; e.g. 2:1.
Useful equations;
    Drawing length = Actual length × Scale ratio
    Actual length = Drawing length / Scale ratio
Example: A component is 200 mm long and is drawn at 1:5.
    Drawing length = 200 × 1/5 = 40 mm.
Important: A scale does not change the actual dimensions of the object. Written dimensions take precedence over measurements taken from a drawing, unless the drawing specifies otherwise.

GEOMETRICAL CONSTRUCTION:
Geometrical construction is the accurate construction of shapes, angles, lines, circles, and curves using drawing instruments or CAD.
Essential constructions;
- Bisecting a line segment.
- Bisecting an angle.
- Constructing perpendicular and parallel lines.
- Constructing standard angles.
- Dividing a line into equal parts.
- Constructing triangles and polygons.
- Drawing tangent lines to circles.
- Constructing arcs and fillets.
- Constructing loci and curves.
Basic equations;
 For a circle:
- Circumference: C = 2πr = πd
- Area: A = πr² = πd²/4
- Diameter: d = 2r
 For a triangle:
- Area: A = ½bh
 For a rectangle:
- Area: A = lw
 For a cylinder:
- Volume: V = πr²h
These equations support dimensional calculations and geometric verification; they do not replace the need for correct geometric construction.

ORTHOGRAPHIC PROJECTION:
Orthographic projection represents a three-dimensional object using two-dimensional views projected perpendicular to the drawing plane.
Principal views;
1. Front view: Shows width and height.
2. Top view (plan): Shows width and depth.
3. Side view: Shows depth and height.
Additional views may be needed when the principal views do not communicate the shape completely.
Projection principles;
- Projectors are perpendicular to the projection plane.
- Corresponding features must align between views.
- Visible edges use continuous thick lines.
- Hidden edges use dashed lines where necessary.
- Circles and features parallel to the viewing plane retain their true shape and size, subject to scale.
 First-angle projection;
- The object is between the observer and the projection plane.
- The top view is placed below the front view.
- The right-side view is placed to the left of the front view.
- The left-side view is placed to the right of the front view.
 Third-angle projection;
- The projection plane is between the observer and the object.
- The top view is placed above the front view.
- The right-side view is placed to the right of the front view.
- The left-side view is placed to the left of the front view.
Exam tip: First-angle and third-angle projection show the same object, but their view arrangements differ. Always check the projection symbol.

ISOMETRIC DRAWING AND PROJECTION:
An isometric drawing represents three dimensions in a single view, with the three principal axes equally inclined to one another in the drawing.
Characteristics;
- The three principal axes are separated by 120°.
- Two principal axes are commonly drawn at 30° to the horizontal, with the third vertical.
- Length, width, and height can be represented in one view.
- Parallel edges remain parallel.
- Circles on inclined isometric planes appear as ellipses in an isometric drawing.
Isometric drawing versus isometric projection
- Isometric drawing: Commonly uses true dimensions along the isometric axes.
- Isometric projection: Uses foreshortened dimensions along those axes.
The isometric scale factor is approximately:
    Isometric length = 0.8165 × True length
This factor applies to true isometric projection, not ordinary isometric drawing.
Applications;
- Visualizing components.
- Explaining assembly and component shape.
- Producing pictorial engineering illustrations.
- Communicating three-dimensional form without multiple views.

OBLIQUE AND PERSPECTIVE DRAWING:
 Oblique drawing;
The front face is drawn in its true shape while receding edges are drawn at an angle.
Types:
- Cavalier: Receding lengths are drawn at full scale.
- Cabinet: Receding lengths are commonly drawn at half scale to reduce distortion.
 Perspective drawing;
Perspective drawing represents objects as they appear to the eye, with parallel receding lines converging toward vanishing points.
Types:
- One-point perspective.
- Two-point perspective.
- Three-point perspective.
Difference: Orthographic projection is primarily used for accurate technical definition, while perspective is useful for realistic visual presentation.

SECTIONAL DRAWING:
A sectional drawing shows internal features by imagining an object cut by a plane and removing the portion between the observer and that plane.
Purpose;
- Reveals internal holes, cavities, ribs, webs, and passages.
- Reduces the need for numerous hidden lines.
- Clarifies complex internal construction.
Types;
- Full section.
- Half section.
- Offset section.
- Broken-out section.
- Revolved section.
- Removed section.
Hatching rules;
- Cut solid material is usually shown by thin, evenly spaced parallel hatch lines.
- Adjacent components may use different hatch directions or spacing.
- Hatching is normally omitted over empty spaces such as holes.
- Standard conventions govern ribs, webs, shafts, fasteners, and other features when sectioned longitudinally.

DIMENSIONING:
Dimensioning specifies the sizes and positions of features required to manufacture or inspect a component.
Main elements;
- Dimension line: Indicates the measured distance.
- Extension line: Connects the dimension to the relevant feature.
- Arrowheads or terminators: Mark dimension limits.
- Dimension value: States the measurement.
- Leader line: Connects a note or symbol to a feature.
Types of dimensions;
- Linear dimensions.
- Angular dimensions.
- Diameter dimensions.
- Radius dimensions.
- Location dimensions.
- Baseline dimensions.
- Chain dimensions.
- Coordinate dimensions.
Rules for dimensioning;
1. Give sufficient dimensions for manufacturing and inspection.
2. Avoid unnecessary repetition of dimensions.
3. Avoid dimensioning to hidden lines where a clearer alternative exists.
4. Place dimensions for easy reading and interpretation.
5. Use Ø for diameters and R for radii.
6. Specify units and apply a consistent convention.
7. Avoid dimension chains that accumulate unacceptable errors.
8. Do not derive manufacturing dimensions by scaling the drawing.
9. Apply tolerances wherever necessary to control variation.
Example;
A shaft may be specified as:
    Ø20 ±0.02 mm
This means the permitted diameter ranges from 19.98 mm to 20.02 mm.

TOLERANCES AND FITS:
A tolerance is the permitted variation in a dimension. It ensures that manufactured parts can function and assemble correctly despite unavoidable manufacturing variation.
Basic equations;
    Upper deviation = Upper limit − Basic size
    Lower deviation = Lower limit − Basic size
    Tolerance = Upper limit − Lower limit
For a symmetric tolerance:
    Maximum size = Basic size + Tolerance/2
    Minimum size = Basic size − Tolerance/2
Example: 50 ±0.1 mm
- Maximum size = 50.1 mm
- Minimum size = 49.9 mm
- Total tolerance = 0.2 mm
Types of fits;
- Clearance fit: Always leaves space between mating parts.
- Transition fit: May produce either small clearance or small interference.
- Interference fit: Requires interference between mating parts.
Fit systems;
- Hole-basis system: The hole's basic lower deviation is zero; different fits are obtained mainly by changing the shaft tolerance position.
- Shaft-basis system: The shaft's basic upper deviation is zero; different fits are obtained mainly by changing the hole tolerance position.
Tolerances may be dimensional, geometrical, or related to surface texture.

LIMITS, GEOMETRICAL TOLERANCING AND SURFACE FINISH:
Geometrical tolerancing controls the form, orientation, location, and runout of features.
Common controls include:
- Form: Straightness, flatness, circularity, cylindricity.
- Orientation: Parallelism, perpendicularity, angularity.
- Location: Position, concentricity or coaxiality concepts where specified by the applicable standard.
- Runout: Circular runout and total runout.
A datum is a theoretically exact reference from which dimensions or geometrical tolerances are established.
Surface texture indicates the condition of a surface after manufacturing. It affects friction, wear, sealing, fatigue performance, and appearance.
The arithmetic mean surface roughness, Ra, is the mean absolute deviation of the assessed surface profile from its mean line over the sampling length, under the specified measurement convention.
For discrete profile measurements:
    Ra ≈ (1/n) Σ|yi|
where yi is each profile deviation from the mean line and n is the number of equally spaced measurements.

AUXILIARY VIEWS AND DEVELOPMENT OF SURFACES
Auxiliary views;
Auxiliary views are used when a surface is inclined to the principal projection planes and its true shape is not visible in the standard views.
They reveal the true size and shape of inclined surfaces.
Development of surfaces;
A development is the flattened pattern of a three-dimensional surface.
Applications:
- Sheet-metal fabrication.
- Ductwork.
- Cones, cylinders, and packaging.
- Fabrication of hoppers and transition pieces.
Common methods:
- Parallel-line development: Prisms and cylinders.
- Radial-line development: Pyramids and cones.
- Triangulation: Transition pieces and irregular shapes.
For a cylinder of diameter d, the circumference is:
    L = πd
This is the length of the rectangular development of the lateral surface, excluding allowances for seams or joining.
For a cone, the slant height is:
    s = √(r² + h²)
The full lateral development is a circular sector of radius s and arc length 2πr. Its central angle is:
    θ = 360° × r/s

SCREW THREADS, FASTENERS AND MACHINE COMPONENTS:
Engineering drawings use standardized conventions for common machine elements.
Screw threads;
Thread designation communicates thread type, nominal diameter, and pitch or other relevant specifications.
Example: M10 × 1.5 indicates a metric thread with a nominal diameter of 10 mm and a pitch of 1.5 mm.
Common components;
- Bolts and nuts.
- Screws and washers.
- Shafts and keys.
- Bearings.
- Springs.
- Gears.
- Couplings.
- Rivets and welds.
Standardized representations simplify drawings. Detailed geometry is provided when necessary for manufacturing, inspection, or assembly.
Assembly drawing;
An assembly drawing shows the relative positions of components and how they fit together.
It may include:
- Item numbers and a bill of materials (BOM).
- Overall and interface dimensions.
- Sectional views.
- Fastening details.
- Exploded views where useful.
A detail drawing gives the complete specifications for manufacturing an individual component.

COMPUTER-AIDED DESIGN (CAD):
CAD uses software to create, modify, analyze, and document engineering designs.
Main CAD functions;
- 2D drafting.
- 3D solid and surface modelling.
- Assembly modelling.
- Dimensioning and annotation.
- Rendering and visualization.
- Engineering simulation and analysis, where supported.
- Drawing generation from 3D models.
Common modelling approaches;
- Sketching: Creates 2D profiles.
- Extrusion: Extends a profile along a direction.
- Revolution: Rotates a profile about an axis.
- Sweep: Moves a profile along a path.
- Loft: Forms geometry between multiple profiles.
- Boolean operations: Combine, subtract, or intersect solids.
CAD good practices;
- Use meaningful file names and revision numbers.
- Apply constraints and dimensions to control sketches.
- Use layers and line types consistently in 2D drawings.
- Use appropriate units and coordinate systems.
- Save native editable files and suitable exchange formats.
- Common formats include DWG and DXF for 2D drafting, STEP for 3D exchange, and STL for mesh-based 3D printing workflows.
- Verify dimensions and tolerances before releasing drawings.

ENGINEERING DRAWING STANDARDS:
Standards ensure that drawings are interpreted consistently across organizations and countries.
Important standards organizations include:
- ISO — International Organization for Standardization.
- ASME — American Society of Mechanical Engineers.
- National standards bodies, which may adopt or adapt international standards.
Relevant standards cover:
- Drawing sheet layouts.
- Line types and lettering.
- Projection methods.
- Dimensioning.
- Geometrical tolerancing.
- Technical product documentation.
- Symbols, surface texture, and tolerances.
Always follow the standard specified by the institution, client, or organization. ISO and ASME conventions are not always identical.

COMMON EXAM AND PRACTICAL TIPS:
1. Identify the required projection method before arranging views.
2. Draw light construction lines before darkening final outlines.
3. Ensure corresponding points align between orthographic views.
4. Use the correct line type for every feature.
5. Distinguish isometric drawing from isometric projection.
6. Include sufficient dimensions without duplication.
7. Use consistent units and scale ratios.
8. Check the geometry and arithmetic of all dimensions.
9. Remember that hidden features may require sectional or auxiliary views.
10. Use tolerances to control functional variation, not merely to make drawings look precise.
11. Check the title block, projection symbol, material, scale, and revision before submission.
12. Practise constructing views from physical objects and visualizing solids from 2D drawings.
