                PRODUCT DESIGN
Product design is the systematic process of identifying user needs and developing products that solve specific problems through the integration of engineering, functionality, aesthetics, materials, technology, and manufacturing considerations.
It transforms an idea or problem into a functional, usable, reliable, and manufacturable product.
  Main objectives;
- Solve real-world problems.
- Meet user and market requirements.
- Improve functionality and performance.
- Ensure safety, reliability, and durability.
- Optimize manufacturing and production costs.
- Improve usability and user experience.
- Minimize environmental impact.
- Support innovation and commercialization.
  Applications;
- Consumer electronics.
- Automotive products.
- Medical devices.
- Industrial machinery.
- Robotics and automation.
- Smart devices and IoT products.
- Household equipment.
- Renewable energy systems.

 TYPES OF PRODUCT DESIGN
  New product design;
Developing a completely new product to solve an existing or emerging problem.
Example: Designing a new autonomous agricultural robot.
  Redesign;
Modifying an existing product to improve its performance, appearance, cost, or functionality.
Example: Redesigning an electric motor to improve efficiency.
  Adaptive design;
Modifying an existing design to suit a different application or operating environment.
Example: Adapting a water pump for solar-powered operation.
  Variant design;
Developing different versions of an existing product using a common design platform.
Example: Producing different sizes of the same machine.
  Innovative design;
Introducing new technologies, materials, mechanisms, or operating principles to create new solutions.
Example: Developing a new energy-storage device.

 PRODUCT DESIGN PROCESS
Product development follows a structured process from identifying a problem to producing and improving the final product.
  Stage 1: Problem identification;
Identify a need, challenge, or opportunity that requires a product solution.
Questions:
- What problem exists?
- Who experiences the problem?
- Why is a solution needed?
- What limitations exist in current solutions?
  Stage 2: Market and user research;
Study potential users, existing products, competitors, and market requirements.
Activities:
- User interviews.
- Questionnaires.
- Observation.
- Competitor analysis.
- Market research.
- Customer feedback.
  Stage 3: Establish design specifications;
Translate user needs into measurable engineering requirements.
Examples:
- Maximum dimensions.
- Required operating speed.
- Load capacity.
- Power consumption.
- Material requirements.
- Safety limits.
- Target manufacturing cost.
- Expected service life.
A good design specification should be clear, measurable, realistic, and testable.
  Stage 4: Concept generation;
Develop multiple possible solutions.
Methods:
- Brainstorming.
- Sketching.
- Morphological analysis.
- Mind mapping.
- Design-by-analogy.
- TRIZ (Theory of Inventive Problem Solving).
  Stage 5: Concept evaluation and selection;
Compare proposed designs against the established requirements.
Common criteria:
- Functionality.
- Cost.
- Performance.
- Manufacturability.
- Safety.
- Reliability.
- Sustainability.
A weighted decision matrix can be used:
    S = Σ(wᵢrᵢ)
Where:
- S = Total concept score.
- wᵢ = Weight assigned to criterion i.
- rᵢ = Rating of the concept against criterion i.
The weights should be normalized so that:
    Σwᵢ = 1
Select the concept with the strongest overall suitability, not necessarily the highest score in one individual category.
  Stage 6: Preliminary design;
Develop the selected concept into an initial engineering design.
Activities:
- Preliminary calculations.
- Material selection.
- Mechanism selection.
- Component layout.
- Basic CAD modelling.
- Initial cost estimation.
  Stage 7: Detailed design;
Define the complete technical characteristics of the product.
Includes:
- 2D engineering drawings.
- 3D CAD models.
- Dimensions and tolerances.
- Material specifications.
- Component specifications.
- Electrical and mechanical designs.
- Assembly details.
- Manufacturing requirements.
  Stage 8: Prototyping;
Build a preliminary physical or digital representation of the product.
Types:
- Concept prototype.
- Visual prototype.
- Functional prototype.
- Engineering prototype.
- Production prototype.
  Stage 9: Testing and validation;
Determine whether the product meets its intended requirements.
Testing may include:
- Functional testing.
- Mechanical testing.
- Electrical testing.
- Thermal testing.
- Environmental testing.
- Safety testing.
- Usability testing.
- Reliability testing.
  Stage 10: Design optimization;
Improve the product using test results and engineering analysis.
Possible improvements:
- Reduce weight.
- Improve efficiency.
- Increase strength.
- Reduce material usage.
- Improve ergonomics.
- Reduce manufacturing costs.
  Stage 11: Manufacturing and commercialization;
Prepare the product for production and market introduction.
Activities:
- Production planning.
- Tooling and equipment selection.
- Quality control.
- Supply-chain planning.
- Packaging.
- Distribution.
- Product documentation.
  Stage 12: Product lifecycle improvement;
Monitor the product after launch and use feedback to improve subsequent versions.

 DESIGN THINKING
Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems through understanding users, generating ideas, prototyping, and testing solutions.
  Five main stages;
1. Empathize: Understand users and their problems.
2. Define: Clearly formulate the problem.
3. Ideate: Generate possible solutions.
4. Prototype: Build representations of proposed solutions.
5. Test: Evaluate solutions with users and improve them.
  Important principles;
- Focus on real user needs.
- Encourage creative thinking.
- Test assumptions early.
- Accept feedback.
- Iterate continuously.
- Combine technical feasibility with user desirability and economic viability.

 ENGINEERING DESIGN CALCULATIONS
Product design requires engineering calculations to ensure that components operate safely and effectively.
  Stress;
σ = F/A
Where:
- σ = Normal stress (Pa).
- F = Applied force (N).
- A = Cross-sectional area (m²).
  Strain;
ε = ΔL/L₀
Where:
- ΔL = Change in length.
- L₀ = Original length.
  Young's modulus;
E = σ/ε
This measures material stiffness within the linear elastic region.
  Factor of safety;
FoS = Failure strength / Working stress
For a design based on yield strength:
FoS = σᵧ/σworking
  Mechanical power;
P = Tω
Where:
- P = Mechanical power (W).
- T = Torque (N·m).
- ω = Angular velocity (rad/s).
For rotational speed N in rpm:
ω = 2πN/60
  Efficiency;
η = (Useful output power / Input power) × 100%
  Electrical power;
For DC circuits:
P = VI
For single-phase AC:
P = VI cosφ
Where φ is the phase angle between voltage and current, assuming sinusoidal operation.
  Energy consumption;
E = Pt
Where:
- E = Energy (J).
- P = Power (W).
- t = Time (s).
  Heat transfer;
Q = mcΔT
Where:
- Q = Heat energy (J).
- m = Mass (kg).
- c = Specific heat capacity (J/kg·K).
- ΔT = Temperature change (K).
These equations are selected according to the product's operating principles and engineering requirements.

 MATERIAL AND COMPONENT SELECTION
Material selection determines the product's strength, weight, durability, cost, and performance.
  Important material properties;
- Strength.
- Hardness.
- Toughness.
- Ductility.
- Stiffness.
- Density.
- Corrosion resistance.
- Thermal conductivity.
- Electrical conductivity.
- Fatigue resistance.
  Material categories
Metals: Steel, aluminium, copper, titanium.
Polymers: ABS, PVC, nylon, polyethylene.
Ceramics: Alumina, silicon carbide, zirconia.
Composites: Fibreglass and carbon-fibre composites.
  Selection considerations;
1. Product operating conditions.
2. Mechanical loading.
3. Environmental exposure.
4. Manufacturing process.
5. Material availability.
6. Cost.
7. Maintenance requirements.
8. Recycling and sustainability.
  Material performance indicators;
Specific strength = Strength / Density
Specific stiffness = Young's modulus / Density
These ratios help compare lightweight materials for applications where mass is important.

 ERGONOMICS AND HUMAN-CENTRED DESIGN
Ergonomics is the study of designing products and systems to suit human capabilities, limitations, and needs.
  Main considerations;
- Comfort.
- Safety.
- Ease of use.
- Accessibility.
- Human body dimensions.
- Hand grip and reach.
- Operating force.
- Visibility.
- User fatigue.
  Applications;
- Tool handles.
- Vehicle controls.
- Computer workstations.
- Medical equipment.
- Industrial machinery.
- Consumer electronics.
  Human factors;
Product designers should consider:
- Anthropometry: Measurement of human body dimensions.
- Biomechanics: Study of human movement and applied forces.
- Cognitive ergonomics: Human perception, memory, and decision-making.
- Physical ergonomics: Posture, movement, and physical interaction.

  Types of prototypes;
Low-fidelity prototype: Simple representation used to explore ideas.
High-fidelity prototype: Detailed representation that closely resembles the final product.
Functional prototype: Tests the actual operating principles.
Appearance prototype: Evaluates appearance, dimensions, and ergonomics.
Engineering prototype: Tests engineering performance and component integration.
  Rapid prototyping technologies;
- FDM 3D printing.
- SLA 3D printing.
- SLS 3D printing.
- CNC prototyping.
- Laser cutting.
- Rapid PCB fabrication.
Advantages
- Detects design errors early.
- Reduces development risk.
- Enables user feedback.
- Supports design iteration.
- Reduces time between concept and testing.

 PRODUCT TESTING AND VALIDATION
Testing determines whether a product performs according to its specifications.
Verification versus validation
Verification: Checks whether the product has been designed and built according to specified requirements.
Question: Are we building the product right?
Validation: Checks whether the product meets actual user needs and intended applications.
Question: Are we building the right product?
  Common tests;
- Load testing.
- Fatigue testing.
- Impact testing.
- Thermal testing.
- Vibration testing.
- Environmental testing.
- Electrical safety testing.
- Usability testing.
- Reliability testing.
  Reliability;
Reliability is the probability that a product performs its required function without failure for a specified period under specified conditions.
For a constant failure rate:
    R(t) = e^(-λt)
Where:
- R(t) = Reliability at time t.
- λ = Constant failure rate.
- t = Operating time.
Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF), for a repairable system under an appropriate constant failure-rate model:
    MTBF = 1/λ 

 DESIGN FOR MANUFACTURING AND ASSEMBLY
  Design for Manufacturability (DFM);
Focuses on simplifying the production of individual components.
Principles:
- Simplify geometry.
- Use standard dimensions.
- Select suitable materials.
- Minimize machining operations.
- Avoid unnecessary precision.
- Consider production volume.
  Design for Assembly (DFA);
Focuses on simplifying the assembly of components.
Principles:
- Reduce component count.
- Standardize fasteners.
- Use self-aligning components.
- Simplify assembly sequences.
- Minimize adjustment operations.
  Design for Reliability (DFR);
Ensures that a product performs consistently throughout its intended service life.
  Design for Maintainability (DFMtn);
Ensures that a product can be inspected, repaired, and serviced efficiently.
  Design for Environment (DfE);
Reduces environmental impact throughout the product lifecycle.

 PRODUCT DESIGN OPTIMIZATION
Design optimization involves improving a product's performance while satisfying engineering and economic constraints.
  Design variables;
Parameters that can be modified.
Examples:
- Material thickness.
- Component diameter.
- Motor power.
- Gear ratio.
- Product weight.
  Objective functions;
Functions that describe what the design should optimize.
Examples:
- Minimize weight.
- Minimize cost.
- Maximize efficiency.
- Maximize strength.
- Minimize energy consumption.
  Constraints;
Conditions that must be satisfied.
Examples:
- Maximum allowable stress.
- Maximum dimensions.
- Minimum safety factor.
- Maximum manufacturing cost.
- Required operating temperature.
A general optimization problem can be expressed as:
Minimize:
    f(x)
Subject to:
    gᵢ(x) ≤ 0
    hⱼ(x) = 0
Where:
- x = Design variables.
- f(x) = Objective function.
- gᵢ(x) = Inequality constraints.
- hⱼ(x) = Equality constraints.
  Common optimization methods;
- Parametric optimization.
- Topology optimization.
- Shape optimization.
- Size optimization.
- Multi-objective optimization.

 PRODUCT COST AND ECONOMIC ANALYSIS
Product design must consider both development costs and long-term production economics.
  Main cost categories;
- Research and development.
- Materials.
- Labour.
- Manufacturing equipment.
- Tooling.
- Assembly.
- Testing and quality control.
- Packaging.
- Transportation.
- Maintenance and warranty.
Unit manufacturing cost
    Unit cost = Total production cost / Number of acceptable units
Break-even quantity
    Q = Fixed cost / (Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit)
Life-cycle cost
    Life-cycle cost = Acquisition cost + Operating cost + Maintenance cost + Disposal cost
This helps evaluate the total economic cost of owning and operating a product.

 SUSTAINABLE PRODUCT DESIGN
Sustainable product design considers environmental, economic, and social impacts throughout a product's lifecycle.
  Important principles;
- Reduce material consumption.
- Improve energy efficiency.
- Use recyclable materials.
- Design for durability.
- Design for repairability.
- Minimize hazardous substances.
- Reduce packaging.
- Design for disassembly.
- Support component reuse.
- Minimize waste and emissions.
  Product lifecycle stages;
1. Raw material extraction.
2. Material processing.
3. Product manufacturing.
4. Packaging and distribution.
5. Product use.
6. Maintenance and repair.
7. End-of-life recovery, recycling, or disposal.
  Life Cycle Assessment (LCA);
LCA evaluates environmental impacts associated with a product throughout its lifecycle.
It generally involves:
- Goal and scope definition.
- Life-cycle inventory.
- Life-cycle impact assessment.
- Interpretation of results.

 PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT MODELS
  Waterfall model
Follows sequential stages, with each major stage generally completed before the next begins.
Suitable for projects with relatively stable requirements.
  Iterative design;
Repeatedly improves a design through cycles of development, testing, feedback, and refinement.
  Agile product development;
Uses short development cycles, frequent feedback, and incremental improvements.
  Stage-Gate model;
Divides product development into stages separated by decision gates.
At each gate, the project is evaluated before proceeding.
  Concurrent engineering
Allows different engineering and business functions to work on overlapping development activities.
This improves coordination and reduces overall development time.

 DESIGN RISK AND FAILURE ANALYSIS
Product design must identify and reduce potential failures before manufacturing and use.
  Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA);
FMEA systematically identifies possible failure modes, their causes, effects, and controls.
Typical columns include:
- Component or process.
- Potential failure mode.
- Failure effect.
- Potential cause.
- Existing controls.
- Severity.
- Occurrence.
- Detection.
Traditional Risk Priority Number:
    RPN = S × O × D
Where:
- S = Severity rating.
- O = Occurrence rating.
- D = Detection rating.
Higher RPN values generally indicate issues requiring attention, although modern risk-based approaches also consider severity directly rather than relying on RPN alone.
  Fault Tree Analysis (FTA);
A top-down method that investigates how combinations of failures can lead to an undesirable system-level event.
Uses:
- AND gates.
- OR gates.
- Basic failure events.
- Intermediate events.

 EXAM AND PRACTICAL TIPS
1. Understand the difference between product design and manufacturing design. Product design develops the complete solution; manufacturing design ensures it can be produced effectively.
2. Always begin with a clearly defined problem and user requirements.
3. Convert user needs into measurable engineering specifications.
4. Generate multiple concepts before selecting a final design.
5. Consider functionality, aesthetics, safety, cost, and manufacturability together.
6. Understand the difference between verification and validation.
7. Use prototypes to identify weaknesses before mass production.
8. Select materials according to loading, operating environment, cost, and manufacturing process.
9. Apply DFM, DFA, DFR, and sustainable design principles.
10. Use engineering calculations and simulations to verify performance.
11. Conduct risk analysis before product release.
12. Maintain proper technical documentation and revision control.
13. Consider the entire product lifecycle, including maintenance and disposal.
14. Use customer feedback to guide continuous improvement.

FINAL PRINCIPLE: 
Successful product design integrates creativity, engineering science, human needs, materials, manufacturing, economics, safety, and sustainability to transform ideas into practical products that solve real-world problems.
